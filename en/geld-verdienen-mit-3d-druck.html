Making money with 3D printing — without your own printer

You design a product, a print factory makes it per order and ships it straight to the customer, you sell it through your own site. No stock, no machine in the basement. Here's how the model really works — and where it gets tricky.

Is this dropshipping? Yes — the better kind

What you're describing has a name: print-on-demand (also on-demand manufacturing). It's a sub-type of dropshipping — you hold no stock, a third party produces per order and ships directly. The decisive difference from classic dropshipping:

  • Classic dropshipping: you resell a supplier's finished catalogue product. Interchangeable, a price war, no edge.
  • Your model: you supply the design yourself. A product of your own that no one else has. Better margin, harder to copy.

That's exactly why it's the stronger model: the value sits in your design, not in reselling mass-produced goods.

The model in three steps

1. Create the design (your STL file)

A 3D printer needs a printable file, usually an STL. Three ways to get there:

  • Draw it the classic way: Tinkercad (free, simple), Fusion, Shapr3D or Blender for more demanding work.
  • Use AI as a starting point: text-to-3D tools like Meshy, Luma Genie, Tripo or Sloyd deliver a rough model in minutes. Honestly: the result is rarely printable straight away — you have to rebuild walls, dimensions and stability. AI saves you the blank canvas, not the manual work.
  • License it: buy ready-made STLs and adapt them (mind the licence).

Important: before anything goes on sale, you print a sample — either yourself or as a single order from the service. What looks good on screen sometimes breaks in reality at the thinnest point.

2. Have it produced and shipped

Instead of printing yourself, you commission a 3D-printing service bureau. It produces per order and ships directly to your customer. For the EU, one thing matters above all: a print and shipping location close to the EU — shorter delivery times, an EU invoice, less customs hassle. Known providers with EU reach include Craftcloud (All3DP), Shapeways, Sculpteo, Treatstock or Xometry. Some even offer ready-made sales shops for your designs.

The sore point is the margin: the service takes its cut, plus material and shipping. That's exactly what you calculate beforehand (see below), otherwise you're working for free.

3. Sell

Two models you shouldn't confuse:

  • Physical (print-on-demand): you sell the printed object through your own shop (Shopify, Etsy) or a marketplace that produces it for you (e.g. the Shapeways marketplace). You ship nothing yourself.
  • Digital (STL licence): you sell only the file for people to print themselves, for instance on Cults3D or MyMiniFactory. No production, no logistics — but the file can be copied, and the market is people with their own printer.

The two can be combined: offer the same design physically and as a file.

Do the maths before you start

The most important table in your business is this one — with your real numbers, not wishful values. An example of how it looks (figures for illustration only, no guarantee):

Selling price (gross)€29.00
− Printing cost (service bureau)− €11.00
− Shipping− €4.50
− Platform/payment fee− €2.50
− VAT (share)− €4.60
= Margin per unit€6.40

If the bottom line shows a break-even or less, the market isn't to blame — the product is too big, too heavy, or sold too cheap. Small, light, custom parts with a clear use add up most reliably.

EU law — please take it seriously:
  • You're the seller: an imprint, a cancellation policy and terms & conditions are mandatory.
  • GPSR: the EU General Product Safety Regulation has applied to small shops too since December 2024 — manufacturer/responsible-person details, safety information.
  • CE/standards: required in addition for certain product groups (e.g. children's toys, electrical) — steer clear if unsure.
  • Taxes: VAT, and possibly the OSS scheme for EU sales.
  • Trademarks & copyright: don't print protected characters, logos or designs — not even if the AI "invented" them.

This is not legal or tax advice. Once there's real revenue, have it checked professionally — it's cheaper than a cease-and-desist letter.

Frequently asked questions

Is this dropshipping?
Yes, a form of it — print-on-demand. No stock, a third party produces per order and ships. The difference: you supply the design yourself, so a product of your own instead of interchangeable catalogue goods. That means better margins and a real edge.
Do I need my own 3D printer?
No. A service bureau prints for you. Your own printer only pays off once volume makes in-house production cheaper — you work that out per product.
What does it cost to start?
Without your own printer the fixed costs are low: design software (often free), a shop fee and sample prints. The sticking point is the unit cost at the service, not the start.
Which products work?
Small, light, customisable things with a clear use or collector value: spare parts, mounts, organisers, decor, personalised gifts. Large or heavy parts kill the margin.
What do I need to consider legally?
An imprint, the right of withdrawal, GPSR, CE if applicable, VAT/OSS. Don't print protected trademarks or characters. Not legal advice — when in doubt, have it checked.
