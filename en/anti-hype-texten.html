Anti-hype copywriting: clichés that weaken your marketing — and what to write instead

Buzzwords sound good and say nothing. Here you'll see why they cost trust, and get concrete before/after examples, a checklist and a few rules of thumb that make your copy clearer straight away.

Why hype costs trust

Most marketing copy doesn't fail because it promises too little. It fails because no one believes it. By the third time someone reads that an offer "changes everything", they stop reading. Overstatement doesn't create excitement, it creates scepticism.

There's a simple reason: hype words carry no verifiable information. "Market-leading" can't be looked up, "effortless" can't be measured, "holistic" means everything and nothing. Your reader notices. They can't always say why a text feels hollow, but they sense it and click away.

Plain words work differently. A concrete statement invites checking, and that very checking builds trust. Whoever writes "saves you about two hours a week on quoting" says more than any superlative — because you can disagree or agree with it. That's the whole trick: you swap assertion for verifiability.

Anti-hype copywriting doesn't mean writing dull copy. It means cutting the words that prove nothing and writing down what's actually true. The result is usually shorter, always clearer and far more credible.

Six clichés and their plain-words replacement

The following examples cover the patterns that appear in almost every marketing text. On the left the typical sentence, on the right a more honest version — and briefly why it works better.

1. Superlatives without proof

BeforeOur "revolutionary" platform is the "best on the market". AfterOur platform combines accounting and invoicing in one tool — you no longer switch between four programs.

A superlative asserts a judgement you can't back up. A concrete feature the reader can assess for themselves — and believe you.

2. Buzzwords with no substance

BeforeWe offer a "holistic", "synergistic" solution and are the "game-changer" for your processes. AfterWe handle three tasks in one step: book appointments, send reminders, refill cancellations.

"Holistic", "synergy" and "game-changer" are placeholders for substance you haven't yet put into words. Once you name the three tasks, you no longer need the buzzword.

3. Exaggerated promises

BeforeIncrease your revenue "guaranteed tenfold" — "effortlessly" and "in no time". AfterCustomers report shorter response times in support. How much that's worth for you depends on your volume.

A guarantee you can't keep is exposed at the first real test. An honest range with a caveat looks less spectacular, but it holds — and you don't lose a customer to disappointed expectations.

4. Filler words

BeforeThis is "actually" a "sort of" fully automatic process that "basically" works pretty well. AfterThe process runs automatically. You only review the exceptions.

"Actually", "sort of" and "basically" weaken every statement they accompany. They signal that you're not sure yourself. Cut them, and the sentence stands.

5. Tangled sentences

BeforeIf you picture yourself on a perfectly ordinary Monday morning, before the first coffee has even brewed, already having to answer the first long email that some customer wrote over the weekend, then you know what I'm talking about. AfterMonday morning, before the first coffee. The first long email from the weekend is already waiting in your inbox. You know what I'm talking about.

One thought per sentence. The long sentence forces the reader to remember the beginning while reading the end. Three short sentences they read without losing the thread.

6. Passive instead of active

BeforeIt is ensured that all requests are handled and the customer is informed. AfterWe answer every request within one working day and let you know as soon as it's done.

The passive hides who's acting. "It is ensured" — by whom? The active names the responsible party and sounds committed. That's the difference between a statement of intent and a promise.

The anti-hype checklist

Run through your copy with this once before you publish. Every box that doesn't tick is a spot to revise.

  • Every judgement is replaced by a verifiable statement or backed up.
  • No superlatives without a source or number behind them.
  • Buzzwords cut, with a concrete description of what happens instead.
  • No promise you couldn't keep in case of doubt.
  • Filler words like "actually", "sort of", "basically" removed.
  • Each sentence carries one thought; long sentences are split.
  • Active not passive — it's clear who's acting.
  • At most one exclamation mark, preferably none.
  • You've read the text aloud and didn't stumble.

Four rules of thumb

  • Verifiable beats great. A number, an example or a concrete result convinces more than any adjective.
  • Would you say it like that? What would sound overblown in conversation sounds hollow written down too. Write the way you speak.
  • Cut before you write. Most copy gets better by taking words away, not adding them. On the first pass, trimming is the most effective edit.
  • One thought, one sentence. If you find an "and" or a "which" in the middle of a sentence, check whether two sentences are hiding in there.

That's the whole toolkit. No secret formula, just discipline: leave out what proves nothing, and write down what's true. Practise it for a few weeks and you'll write more clearly without thinking about it.

Frequently asked questions

Are buzzwords in marketing really bad?

They aren't wrong, they're empty. Words like "holistic" or "synergy" carry no verifiable information. Cut them and replace them with a concrete statement, and you lose nothing and gain credibility. They only become harmful when they hide a real claim.

How do I spot hype in my own copy?

Three questions help: can the claim be verified? Is there a number or evidence, or just a superlative? Would you say the sentence out loud in a conversation? If you can't back up a claim and it would sound overblown spoken aloud, it's hype. The Hype Filter flags the typical patterns automatically.

How do I write convincingly without overstating?

With concrete statements instead of judgements. Say what your offer does, for whom, and with what measurable result. Active not passive, short sentences, one thought per sentence. Conviction comes from verifiability, not from adjectives.
