Chapter 12 - The Dyke Book

The war ended in 1918, and it ended for the marsh as everywhere: in exhaustion, in scarcity, in a stillness that was no relief. Thies Feddersen came back, other than he had gone, more silent, harder, with eyes that had seen too much. He took up his work at the farm again, and Hauke again gave him the good wages, and between the two men lay the same silence as before the war, only grown deeper.

In that winter, when everything returned to the old, poorer order, Okke Nissen came to the Carstens warft. The dyke-warden had grown old, over seventy, his hands trembled, and he could no longer serve the office. He came to hand over the dyke book, the old book in which one had entered for a hundred years what happened at the dyke, and which now passed into the dyke-reeve's keeping, as was the custom when a dyke-warden laid down his office.

Okke laid the book on the table, the heavy, leather-bound book, and his trembling hands rested on it a moment. "It's all in there," he said. "A hundred years of dyke. Every flood, every repair, every inspection." He looked at Hauke, and in the leathery, aged face was something Hauke could not interpret and yet understood. "That of nineteen-hundred-six too. The faulty spot. That it was patched and not done. That a warning was given." A pause. "It's in there, Hauke. I entered it, back then, as was my duty. I couldn't leave it out. But I also told no one. That was my sin, if you will. That I entered it and then kept silent."

Hauke looked at the book. "Why did you say nothing? To the authority, back then. You could have —"

"I know what I could have done." Okke took his hands off the book. "I could have brought you down. Split the koog, in the year we had to hold together to survive. I didn't do it. Out of loyalty, I told myself. Out of cowardice, I tell myself now, old as I am and not long for it. It was both." He looked at Hauke. "But the book I wrote as it was. That was the one thing I did right. What you do with it is your affair. You can tear out the page. No one would notice. Or you let it stand."

And Okke went, slowly, with the trembling hands, out into the winter, and Hauke stayed alone with the book.

That night he opened it, by the light of the lamp, and leafed until he found the page. There it stood, in Okke's old, orderly hand: the date, the faulty spot by the sluice, the note that it had been patched and not thoroughly done, that the dyke-reeve had been warned. Black on white, the truth that no one knew, that no one had read except Okke and now Hauke.

He held the book nearer the lamp. He could have torn out the page. It would have been sensible; it would have erased the last trace, the last testimony. He took the page between his fingers. And then he left it. He left it standing, the page, the entry, the truth, and he did not quite know why, only that it was impossible for him to destroy it, as it had been impossible for him to speak the truth. Perhaps it was cowardice, like Okke's. Perhaps it was something else, something that was almost hope: that one distant day there would be someone who opened the book, who read, who asked, and that then there should be something that answered, when all the mouths that now kept silent had long fallen quiet. He could not say it. But he could let the entry stand, for the one who would come.

He closed the book and laid it in the chest, with the other farm papers, and outside it was still, quite still, because frost had covered the land and even the water at the sluice had come to rest. Bo, now ten, slept in the next room, and in the morning he would go again to the field and to the dyke, the boy who would one day inherit the book, with the chest, with the entry, without knowing what he inherited. And Hauke sat long by the light of the lamp and looked at the chest in which the truth now lay, enclosed, kept, passed on, and he thought that one does not get rid of a guilt by walling it up, as little as one gets rid of the sea by building a dyke. Both remain. Both press. And one day both find a spot where one has saved.
