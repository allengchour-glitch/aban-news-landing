Chapter 14 - The Old Dyke-Reeve

Hauke grew old, as Okke had grown old, and the time came when he had to give up the office. He had held it nearly thirty years, longer than most, and he had held it well, everyone said so: a conscientious dyke-reeve who left no spot weak, who would rather over-repair than under, who never saved at the dyke. They did not know where this conscientiousness came from, and Hauke did not say.

Bo strove for the office. He was a man now, in his mid-twenties, soon to marry, and he wanted to be what his father had been, and more: he wanted to be greater. The ambition Hauke had seen sprouting in the child had now fully blossomed, and it was uncanny to Hauke, because he knew it so exactly.

There was a last dyke inspection that Hauke and Bo walked together, the old dyke-reeve and the coming one, section by section, and Hauke showed his son everything, every spot, every weakness, every history of the dyke. And when they came to the spot by the sluice, the spot that was now for thirty years the strongest of the whole dyke, because Hauke had made it higher and firmer after the flood than everything else — there Hauke halted, and something in him wanted to speak.

"This spot," he said. "Bo. This spot you must always keep an eye on."

"It's the strongest on the whole dyke," said Bo. "One can see that."

"Yes. Now it is." Hauke looked at the spot, at the firm, high mountain beneath which the water lay, at which a man had once drowned. "It wasn't always the strongest. There was a time when it was the weakest. There —" He faltered. The sentence lay on his tongue, the whole sentence, the truth: There the dyke broke, because I had not had the spot done, and Momme Feddersen drowned, and on that our farm stands. It lay on his tongue, at the place itself, before the son who would inherit the office and with the office the responsibility for the spot.

"There what?" asked Bo.

Hauke looked at the son, the ambitious, capable son who knew nothing, and he could not get it out. "There it was weak," he said. "Long ago. Hold the dyke, Bo. Nothing more. Hold it well. Never save at the dyke. That's all I can give you."

It was all, and it was too little, and neither knew it, the one because he did not know what was missing, the other because he could not say it. They walked the inspection to its end, and Bo was elected dyke-reeve the same year, young for the office, as Hauke had been young, and Hauke watched him take the office with a mixture of pride and fear that he could explain to no one.

Hauke died a few years later, in bed, in winter, an old man who could no longer walk the dyke. Bo sat with him in the last days, and once, in a clear moment, Hauke reached for the son's hand and said: "Bo. There is something you must know. About the flood, back then. About Momme Feddersen. About the spot —"

And then his strength left him, or his courage, or both, and the clear moment passed, and Hauke sank back and said only: "Hold the dyke. And the book — the book in the chest —" No more came. The sentence he had not said for thirty years stayed unfinished even now, at the edge of death, as it had stayed unfinished at the spot by the sluice. Hauke Carstens died with his guilt, almost whole, and took the essential with him, and left behind only the breach, unnamed, inherited, and the dyke book in the chest, with the entry no one read.

Bo did not understand the last words. The book in the chest — he found it, leafed through it, saw a hundred years of dyke history, floods, repairs, inspections, and he found the entry of 1906 too, read it, and did not understand it, because he did not know what it pointed to; for Bo it was an old entry about an old spot that had long been the strongest. He shut the book and laid it back in the chest, and he inherited his father's silence, completely, without knowing the ground of it, and laid it, without wanting to, over his own children, who would come.
