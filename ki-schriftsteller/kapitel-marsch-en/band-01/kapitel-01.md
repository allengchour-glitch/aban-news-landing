Chapter 1 - The New Dyke-Reeve

They elected Hauke Carstens dyke-reeve on a March day in 1901, and there were those in the koog who thought him too young for it, thirty-one, and did not say so aloud but only saw it, the way in the marshland one sees everything and says little. Hauke knew it. He saw it in the faces as he stepped out of the inn into the wet March air, and he took it with him, the way he took everything, without expression.

The land lay flat beneath the sky, so flat that the sky was the greater thing and the land only a dark line beneath it. Whoever grew up here learned early that there was no above to hold to, only the wide expanse and the wind that came from the west and never quite stopped. Over the marsh the dwelling-mounds lay like islands, the warften, artificial hills onto which the farmsteads had been set, high enough that the water would not reach them at flood tide, and on the largest mound of the Sankt-Annen-Koog stood the Carstens farm, as it had for four generations, and Hauke was the fifth.

The day after the election he walked the dyke with old Okke Nissen, who had been dyke-warden as long as Hauke could remember. Okke was a bony man with a face like untanned leather, and he walked slowly, with the deliberateness of one who knows the dyke does not run off and neither does the water. They walked on the crown of the dyke, and to their right lay the won land, the marsh, fat and fertile, with its ditches and sluice-drains, and to their left lay the water, grey and patient, behind the foreland and the brushwood groynes that had been built out into it to wrest the silt from the sea.

"There," said Okke, and pointed with his chin at a spot at the dyke's foot where the grass grew differently, darker, more lush. "There the water stands too long. It seeps through. You must remember that, Hauke. The dyke talks, if you listen. The grass tells you where it grows tired."

Hauke looked. He saw it now that Okke showed it: the darker line, the rich green that meant nothing good, for here only what had too much water grew lush. "What does it cost to put right?"

Okke looked at him from the side, and in the leathery face something moved that was almost a smile and never became one. "That's what you ask first. What it costs." He walked on. "Your father always asked the price first too. And your grandfather. It's the question of the Carstenses."

"It's a reasonable question."

"It's a reasonable question," said Okke. "Until it isn't."

They walked the whole dyke, three hours, and Okke showed him everything, every spot where the mountain — that was what the old people called the dyke, the mountain, as if there were mountains up here — every spot where the mountain was weak, where it held, where they had patched last year. Hauke listened and remembered it all, for that was what a dyke-reeve had to be able to do: know the dyke like his own body, every spot, every weakness, every cracking. The office was not an honour, even if the people called it so. The office was responsibility for the lives of all who lived behind the dyke, and whoever forgot that learned it in the first heavy tide.

At home, on the warft, Antje was waiting. She stood in the doorway of the great farmhouse, one hand at the small of her back, for the first child was on the way, and she watched him come up the mound path, slowly, with the gait of one who has been three hours on the dyke. She did not say she was glad. She said: "How does the dyke stand?"

"It stands," said Hauke. "At one spot the water stands too long. By the sluice. Okke wants me to put it right."

"Then put it right."

"It costs."

Antje looked at him, and her gaze was clear and without detour, as it always was, and Hauke had learned that this gaze saw more than he liked. "Everything costs," she said. "A dead man costs more." She stepped aside and let him in, into the warmth of the farmhouse, into the smell of cattle and peat and boiled cabbage, and Hauke went in, and outside, behind the dyke, the water rose to the flood, as it rose twice a day, as it had since the world began, and sank again, patient, and each time took a little silt and laid down a little silt, and no one could see by looking what it intended.

In the night Hauke lay awake and listened to the water. You could hear it, when the wind stood right, the working of the tide at the sluice, a sucking and pressing, and Hauke lay and heard it and reckoned. He reckoned what the spot by the sluice would cost, done properly, with new clay and faggots, and he reckoned what it cost to patch it, cheaply, for this year. And he thought that a heavy tide came rarely, every ten, every twenty years, and that one could put right a great deal by then, and that the money saved now would benefit the farm, the child that was coming. He thought that, and he knew at the same time that Okke was right, that it was a reasonable question, until it wasn't. But the tide was far off, and the child was near, and Hauke fell asleep before he had decided, and believed he had decided nothing.
