Chapter 8 - Who Dykes, Gains

The koog had to be dyked in again, and Hauke did it, with an energy that was almost obsession. He was everywhere, from the first day: organised the closing of the breach, procured clay and faggots, divided the men, worked himself, to exhaustion. The people saw it and said the young dyke-reeve took the flood to heart, he worked as if he wanted to make the disaster un-happen with his own hands, and that did him credit. They did not know that they were right, only differently than they meant: Hauke worked against something that could not be worked away, and the more he worked, the less it helped, for the work was no atonement, it was only a numbing.

They closed the breach, and they made the dyke higher and stronger than it had been, higher than the new mark the flood of 1906 had set. That was the lesson the koog drew from every flood: one built the dyke to the last flood, always one flood too late, for the next would be higher again, that was the old game between the human and the water, a game the human never won and never quite lost.

And then came the new survey. For a part of the land could not be reclaimed, at least not at once. The deepest stretch of the koog, where the breach had been and the water had stood longest, stayed wet, salted, useless, and it was decided to lead the new dyke a stretch inland and leave this deepest stretch outside for now, to the water, as foreland. It was a sensible decision, a technical one, and it had a consequence no one said aloud: in this abandoned stretch lay the Feddersen warft. Momme's farm. His land.

So the Feddersen widow lost what had remained to her. The farm was flooded anyway, the warft half washed away, but the land that belonged to it, which now lay outside the new dyke, that was worth nothing any more, was foreland, given over to the water. And through the same survey the Carstens farm gained: land that had once bordered the Feddersens fell now, through the new dyke line, to the Carstens side, to Hauke's farm. It was no intention, Hauke told himself, it was the geography, the technology, the most sensible line for the new dyke. And yet the result was that Hauke gained where Momme died, that his farm grew larger out of the flood that his spot had broken.

Hauke tried to help the widow. He sent her corn, secretly, through a maid, and money, anonymously, he thought. The widow sent the corn back. She came herself, on a winter day, across the muddy mound path, a woman who had aged twenty years in one night, and she set the sack of corn before Antje's door and said, when Antje opened: "We take nothing from the house of Carstens." She said it calmly, almost toneless. "My husband stood at your spot. At the spot he warned of. You know that, Antje Carstens. You know it as well as I do." And she went, without another word, and left the sack standing, and Antje picked it up and closed the door and said nothing, and from that day the women did not greet each other, who had once washed together at the sluice.

The widow knew, then. Perhaps Momme had told her, before the flood, of the warning and the not-being-heard; perhaps she knew it only with the certainty of women who read their men's silences like the weather. She had no proof, as little as Antje had proof, as little as little Thies Feddersen, Momme's son, who was now fatherless and impoverished. But she knew, and her knowing was an accusation that weighed the more because it never came to court, but lived only in a returned sack of corn, in an unreturned greeting, in the knowledge of two families that would inherit like the land itself.

At night Antje laid her hand on Hauke's back, in the dark, and Hauke lay awake and listened to the water, which now stood behind a higher dyke, further off, but not gone, never quite gone. He had gained. His farm was larger, his dyke stronger, his office secured, for a dyke-reeve who rebuilt so vigorously after the flood counted for something. He had gained everything there was to gain, and he knew he had lost it, in the same moment he gained it, and that no higher dyke and no larger land ever again balanced this one reckoning.
