Chapter 11 - Antje's Question

The hunger winter of 1917 struck the marsh like everyone, and in that winter Hauke again gave more than he could, of the little the farm had. He carried it away, in the dark, as he had for years, to the Feddersen widow, to other needy ones, and he believed no one noticed, least of all Antje.

But Antje noticed everything. She ran the economy, she counted the corn, she knew what was missing. One evening, when he again came home with a half-empty sack, she confronted him. She did not do it loudly. She stood at the hearth, stirring the thin gruel, and said, without turning round: "Who are you atoning for, Hauke?"

It was the same word that another woman in another land in another winter would say to another man, and Hauke did not know it and yet felt it: it was the word that named everything. Atoning. He was silent.

"You carry the corn to the Feddersen widow," said Antje. "For the third time this month. While your own children grow thinner at the table. Why, Hauke?"

"She's alone. Her husband is dead. Her son in the war."

"Her husband is dead." Antje turned round, the spoon in her hand, and her gaunt face was hard in the glow of the hearth-fire. "Yes. Her husband is dead. He stood at the spot by the sluice, Hauke. At the spot you knew would not hold. Of which Momme warned you. Which you should have had done in autumn and did not." She said it calmly, almost without accusation, as if she were reading something long written. "That's what you atone for. Not from pity. From guilt."

It was the first time in eleven years that she spoke it, the one thing that stood between them since the flood night. She had always known it — since the inspection, since Momme's visit in autumn, since the assigning of the watches, since the questioning by the authority at which Hauke kept silent. She had known it and never said it, and now she said it, half, in their own parlour, in the hunger winter, and it lay between them like a stone one finally names.

Hauke could have said everything now. He could have said: Yes. I knew. I saved money, and a man died for it, and I carry it every day. He could have said it to Antje, the only one before whom he need not dissemble, because she knew it anyway. But he did not say it. He said: "It's war, Antje. The widow has no one. Should I let her starve?"

It was no answer, and Antje knew it was none. "Give her what you must," she said and turned back to the hearth. "But don't tell me it's pity. I know you, Hauke. You atone. And atoning makes nothing good. It only makes it bearable for you." She stirred the gruel. "The dead man stays dead, whether you bring the widow corn or not. The only thing you'd owe her is the truth. And that you don't give her. Rather the corn than the truth. That's how you are."

It was the hardest and truest thing she had ever said to him, and Hauke carried it off, as he carried everything off. He went out into the night, before the door of the farmhouse, and stood in the wind that came from the west, and heard the water far behind the dyke. And he understood that Antje now knew fully what he knew, and that she carried it all the same, in silence, that she had settled into the carrying, the way the land settles into being embanked, behind a wall that holds and yet is never quite safe. She had become the silent woman who knows everything. And Hauke knew that he had made her so, and that too he carried, on top of everything else.
