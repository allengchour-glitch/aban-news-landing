Chapter 5 - The Spring Tide

The winter of 1906 came early and hard, and at the beginning of December the wind turned to northwest and stayed there. That was the dangerous thing, not the storm alone, but the wind that stood, day after day, for a standing northwest pressed the water of the North Sea into the bight, pressed it against the coast, higher and higher, and when then a spring tide came as well, the high tide at new or full moon, the water piled up over all the marks one knew.

Okke Nissen read the weather like a book, and on a day in December he came to the Carstens warft, without his cap, his thin hair in the wind, and his leathery face was grey. "One's coming," he said. "A heavy one. The wind's stood four days, and the day after tomorrow is spring tide. That'll give one such as we haven't had in a long time."

Hauke felt the cold come over him, and it was not the wind. "How high?"

"Higher than the marks. How high, only the water knows. But high." Okke looked at him. "The spot by the sluice, Hauke."

"I know."

"It won't hold that."

"I know, Okke." Hauke said it sharply, more sharply than he meant, and Okke only nodded and said no more, for what was there to say now. Now it was too late to do the spot; one could not in two days, in this weather, properly mend a dyke section. One could only watch it, in the night, with men and sandbags and faggots, and hope it held, or, if it gave way, prevent the worst.

Hauke called up the whole koog. That was the right and the duty of the dyke-reeve: at a threatening storm tide every man had to come to the dyke, every one, without exception, for a breach hit all. They came, in the dusk of the storm day, with spades and lanterns, in oilskins, and Hauke divided them, section by section, and it was a strange hour, in which the whole koog stood at the dyke, rich and poor, Carstens and Feddersen and all the others, united against the one thing that threatened them all.

He divided the watches, and when he came to the spot by the sluice, the faulty spot, he needed a man who understood the water, one who would recognise when it grew critical, one to whom one could entrust the most dangerous spot. And without long thought, half from reason, half from something else he did not admit to himself, he said: "Momme. You take the spot by the sluice. You know it. You know what to watch for."

Momme looked at him. It was a long look, and in it lay much: the knowledge that this was the most dangerous spot; the knowledge that Hauke had been warned and not heeded; and something like bitterness, that now of all men he should guard the spot he had warned against. "The spot I said would break," said Momme.

"It holds best if there's one there who knows it."

"Or it takes the one who's there." Momme said it without reproach, almost calmly, a statement. Then he took his spade. "Good. I'll take the spot." He went there, through the storm, to the faulty spot by the sluice, and Hauke watched him go, and in him was a pull he did not want to name.

Antje, hauling sandbags with the other women, had heard the assignment. She came to Hauke, the kerchief tight around her head, and said into his ear, against the storm: "You're sending Momme to the spot."

"He knows it best."

"You're sending him to the spot you know can break." She looked at him, and in her eyes was something Hauke had never seen there before, a fright that went deeper than the fear of the flood. "Hauke."

"It's the best assignment," said Hauke, and it did not sound convinced, and Antje heard it. But there was no time left, for the flood rose, faster now, and the gauge at the sluice already stood over the first mark, and rose further, over the second, and the wind howled across the dyke and tore the words from the men's mouths, and the water came, grey and black and higher than anything the living knew, and pressed against the dyke, against the spot that had not been done, and Momme Feddersen stood before it, with his lantern, alone in the storm, and guarded it.
