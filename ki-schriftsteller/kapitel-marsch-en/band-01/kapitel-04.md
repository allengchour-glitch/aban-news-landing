Chapter 4 - The Faulty Spot

Autumn came, and with it the harvest, and the harvest bound all hands, as Hauke had foreseen and secretly hoped. It was a good year, a full one, the corn stood heavy, and one could not harvest and dig at the dyke at once, everyone saw that, that was no idleness, that was the reason of the marsh. So the spot by the sluice was not done, not in autumn, not properly, as Hauke had promised before the inspection. It stayed as it was, patched, and Hauke told himself one would do it the next spring, certainly, first of all, before anything else.

But Momme Feddersen did not forget the spot. He went there, of his own accord, on a day in October, and looked at it, and that evening he came to Hauke on the warft, which he rarely did, and stood in the doorway of the farmhouse, his cap in his hand, and said: "The spot is getting worse."

"It's harvest time, Momme."

"The harvest is nearly in. In two weeks the hands are free." Momme took a step inside, and the lamplight fell on his face, which was serious, more serious than usual. "Hauke, I'm not telling you this because I wanted the office and didn't get it. That's over, I don't hold it against you. I'm telling you because I've seen the spot. The water comes through now already, at an ordinary tide. The clay won't hold any more. If a heavy tide comes, this winter, it'll break there. Not somewhere else. There."

Hauke stood at the table, and Antje sat by the hearth and listened without looking up, and sewed, and Hauke felt that she was listening, and that made it worse. "We'll do it in spring," he said. "Properly. I promise you."

"In spring the tide season is over. In spring it's easy to do it, because the danger is past. That's no art, Hauke." Momme looked at him, and in his gaze there was now something hard. "The art is to do it now, before the winter, where it's difficult and expensive and where the hands are needed elsewhere. That's the office. Not the easy spot at the easy time. The hard one at the hard time."

"I'm the dyke-reeve, Momme."

"Yes," said Momme. "You are." He put his cap back on. "Then decide as dyke-reeve. But don't decide for your farm. Decide for the koog. That's the difference, and you'll learn it, one way or another." He nodded to Antje, curtly, and went out into the autumn night, and Hauke heard his steps fade across the mound path.

For a while it was quiet in the farmhouse. Then Antje said, without looking up, without stopping the needle: "He's right."

"It costs two weeks' work, now, when —"

"He's right, Hauke. You know it. You know it better than he does, for you have the book and the inspection and the responsibility. You know the spot won't hold. And you don't do it, because it's expensive and because the tide may not come." Now she looked up, and her gaze met his, clear and without mercy. "May not. You're staking the koog on a may-not. That's what you're doing."

"A heavy tide comes once in twenty years."

"And if it comes this year?"

Hauke had no answer to that, and that was the answer. He went to the door and looked out, into the night, across the flat land toward the dyke one did not see, only sensed, and behind it the water one heard when the wind stood. He thought: If I do the spot now, I give Momme the right of it, then the passed-over man has schooled the new dyke-reeve, before the whole koog, for the whole koog would know. And he thought: If I don't do it and it goes well, then I've saved money and kept my authority. And if it does not go well — but that he did not think to the end. He left the thought at the edge, where it did not hurt, the way one leaves an entry in the book at the edge, unread.

"In spring," he said once more, quietly, more to himself than to Antje.

Antje said nothing more. She sewed on, and the child in her, that would come soon, kicked against her hand, and she laid her hand a moment on her belly, and Hauke saw it and turned away. Outside the wind went through the night, from the west, and carried up the working of the tide, the sucking and pressing at the sluice, at the spot that had not been done, and no one slept well that night on the Carstens warft, and outside, in the dark, the water came and went and remembered, tide by tide, where the man had saved.
