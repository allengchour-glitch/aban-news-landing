Chapter 15 - The Hereditary Farm

Bo led the farm into a time that was changing, faster than the marsh was used to. In the early thirties came first the crisis, the collapse of prices, the hardship even on the land, and then came the new time, with flags and promises and a law that seemed made precisely for the farmers of the marsh: the Reich Hereditary Farm Law.

The hereditary farm. It meant the farm was indivisible, unsellable, unmortgageable, that it passed undivided to a single heir, that no bank could take it, no creditor. For a man like Bo, who set the farm above all else, who wanted to increase and hold it at any price, it was a promise he could scarcely resist: protection, security, honour, the farmer as bearer of the people, the soil as sacred. It fit everything Bo was and wanted to be, and he threw in his lot with those who promised it, without asking long what else he was making common cause with.

He had married Grete, a farmer's daughter from the neighbouring koog, clear and incorruptible like Antje once, and Grete saw the new time with other eyes than Bo. She mistrusted the flags, the promises, the noise. But she held it back, for the children's sake, and because a woman in the marsh had little say against a man who had the office and the farm and now the party behind him.

Thies Feddersen was still there, the last Feddersen, landless, a labourer, now at Bo's, as he had been at Hauke's. The old humiliation went on under a new sign: where the hereditary farm law raised and protected the farmer, it left the landless precisely what he was, without prospect, without land, for a hereditary farm could only be held by one who already had land. Thies saw Bo rise in the new time, as he had seen Hauke rise after the flood, and his face betrayed nothing, and he worked and was silent.

Bo took over from his dead father the dyke book too, which lay in the chest. He took it out, once, leafed through it, saw the entry of 1906, which he had already seen once and not understood. This time he read it more closely: the faulty spot by the sluice, patched and not done, the dyke-reeve warned. And for a moment an inkling brushed him, a chill, a connection that did not quite fit — his father, the office, the flood in which Momme Feddersen drowned, Thies's father, who now worked for him. But Bo was not a man who pursued inklings that were uncomfortable. He shut the book, laid it back, and told himself it was an old entry about an old spot that had long been the strongest on the dyke. The father had made it so strong precisely for that reason. What should be amiss in it.

So Bo overlooked the truth that was put into his hand, because he could not interpret it and would not, and laid the book back in the chest, and with it the guilt he now carried without knowing it. And over the marsh flew the new flags, and the hereditary farm law made the Carstens farm untouchable, grown out of a flood, founded on a concealed breach, and no one asked where the prosperity came from, least of all Bo, who now increased it under the protection of the new time.
