Chapter 7 - An Act of God

The authority came from the county town, three days after the flood, when the water was slowly beginning to run off again, through the breach and through the sluices, and the land emerged from the sea once more, muddy, devastated, with dead animals and broken fences and a thick layer of silt over everything. A hydraulic inspector came and an official from the district administrator, and they recorded the breach, as one recorded such breaches: soberly, measuring, with the wish to find a cause that struck no one.

They found what they sought. A spring tide at standing northwest, a level that exceeded all known marks, a force of nature against which no dyke in the world was safe. An act of God. That was the formula, and it fit, for it was half true: the tide had really been exceptional, the level really beyond all marks. That the dyke broke at precisely the weakest spot, at the spot one knew and had not done — that could be explained otherwise too, and the inspector, who did not know the koog, had no reason to ask otherwise.

Hauke gave his statement. He gave it as dyke-reeve, terse, in firm sentences, and he told the truth, as far as it served him. He said the tide had exceeded all marks. He said the dyke had been in proper condition after the last inspection. He said one had done what was possible. What he did not say — and only Antje, who was permitted to be present because she was the dyke-reeve's wife, heard the silence around this one spot — was that he had known of the weakness by the sluice. That Okke had advised a thorough repair. That Momme had warned, twice, three times. That there was an entry in the dyke book. He said nothing of it, and the concealing was no lie in the strict sense, for no one asked him; it was only the leaving-out of what would have struck him, and that was almost worse, because it was so easy.

Then it was Okke's turn. Okke Nissen, the dyke-warden, who kept the dyke book in which the entry stood. The inspector asked him about the condition of the dyke, and Okke, who could not lie and would not lie, said the dyke was old and at many a spot tired, as every dyke was. The inspector asked whether there had been known weak spots. And Okke —

Okke was silent a moment too long. Hauke saw it, saw the old man wrestle, saw how the knowledge lay on his tongue, the entry, the warning, the faulty spot. One word, and it would have been out, and Hauke would have been a man of acts of God no longer, but a dyke-reeve who out of thrift had not done a spot at which a man drowned. One word.

Okke said: "Weak spots, every dyke has. You inspect them, you patch them. More a man cannot do against the water." He did not look at Hauke as he said it. He looked at his hands, the old, spotted hands that had kept the book, and he did not lie, for what he said was true — and he did not say the whole truth, for he named neither the entry nor the warning nor the one spot. It was loyalty, perhaps. It was fear, perhaps, of what it meant to accuse the dyke-reeve, to split the koog in a time when the koog had to hold together to survive. It was the reason of the marsh, which made silence cheaper than speech. Whatever it was, Okke now carried it too, and with him the dyke book, in which the truth stood black on white and which no one opened.

Momme's death was recorded as an accident in the dyke service. A brave man, it said, who did his duty at an endangered spot and was taken by the water. It was true, every word, and it left out the one thing: that the spot was endangered because one had not done it, and that the one who knew this was the man who had stationed him there. Momme's widow received a small compensation, as was due to the bereaved, and the authority departed, and the koog stayed with its devastation and its dead man and its formula: an act of God.

In the evening, when it was all over, Hauke and Antje sat in the parlour, and neither said anything. Then Antje looked at him, long, and in her gaze there was no accusation, but something heavier: the knowledge that they now belonged together, more firmly than by the marriage certificate, welded together by what he had done and concealed and she had not prevented. She could have stood up, could have gone to the authority, could have named the entry in the book. She did not. She stood up, stoked the fire, hung the pot over it, and said: "Eat. It was a long day." And that was her part of the silence, her own, and they laid it together over what had happened, the way the water laid the silt over the devastated land, thick and smooth and covering everything.
