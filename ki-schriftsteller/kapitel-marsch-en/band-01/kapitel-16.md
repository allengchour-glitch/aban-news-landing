Chapter 16 - Blood and Soil

The slogans of the new time seemed made for the marsh. Blood and soil, it was called, the farmer as the root of the people, the soil as sacred, the land as the inheritance of the forebears. For a farmer whose family had wrested the same soil from the sea for five generations, that did not sound like a lie but like a confirmation of what he believed anyway: that the land was everything, that the farm counted, that the farmer was something special. Bo let himself be carried by these words, because they flattered him, because they gave him what his ambition hungered for: the feeling of being something.

He became local farm leader. It was no great office, but it was power, small power over the neighbours, over the delivery quota, over the distribution, and Bo misunderstood it, as his father had once half misunderstood the dyke-reeve's office: as a rise, a duty, what was due to one who was capable. He did not see, or did not want to see, what he carried along by holding the office, what system he served by keeping lists and collecting quotas and putting himself on the side of the loud.

The first severities came, quietly at first. A neighbour who had talked too openly was fetched and came back changed. A pedlar in the county town, a Jew, was one day no longer there. One learned to lower the gaze, to see nothing, to say nothing — the old art of the marsh that Bo had inherited from his father without knowing he had inherited it, this art of looking away, of protecting one's own, of holding oneself out, while others fell. Bo looked away, as Hauke had looked away, and called it reason, called it duty for the farm, for the family.

Grete brought a child into the world that year, a son, and for one summer happiness seemed possible: the farm, the children, the fertile marsh, the garden behind the warft where Grete grew beans and cabbage. It was a real happiness, not a pretended one, a summer of sun and growth. But over the happiness lay what lay over everything in that time: the flags, the slogans, the war that was not yet there and already stood on the horizon, the way a storm tide can be read in the weather days before.

And beneath the happiness lay the soil that bore the guilt. Bo did not know it. He stood on the dyke, on an evening in that last summer of peace, and looked over his land, the won land, the fat, fertile land of the marsh, and before the dyke lay the abandoned foreland, under water, with the sunken Feddersen warft that no one saw any more, that only the old people knew. Bo saw the calm water over the foreland and thought nothing of it, thought of the harvest, of the farm, of the son who slept. He did not know that he stood on a guilt half a century old, and that he would carry it on into a time darker than anything the marsh had known, and that the water, which lay so calm over the old foreland, waited patiently, as it always waited, for a time not yet born.

Behind him, in the chest, lay the dyke book with the entry he had overlooked. Before him lay the water over the taken land. And in him, unknown, lay the breach his father had made, and now rode with him, with Bo Carstens, local farm leader and dyke-reeve, into the war and into his own guilt, which was yet to come and would so resemble the first that it was as if the water had written it a second time.
