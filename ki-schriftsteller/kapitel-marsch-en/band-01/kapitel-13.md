Chapter 13 - Inflation and Land

The money died in the early twenties, and it died in the marsh too, although the marsh was far from the cities where one carried the notes in a washing basket. Hauke had saved, in the years as dyke-reeve, in the good harvest years; he had set aside what he could, a cushion for the farm, for Bo's future. And it evaporated, almost overnight, became worthless paper, as everywhere.

But unlike a man in the city who had nothing but his money, Hauke had the land. And the land remained. It could not be devalued, not printed worthless overnight; it lay where it lay, fat and fertile, the won land of the marsh, and with it the stretch that had fallen to him out of the flood, through the new dyke line, the land that had once bordered the Feddersens. While the money of the rise evaporated, the ground of the rise remained: the land he had gained where Momme lost.

Antje saw him sitting at the table with the worthless notes, and she said the sentence Hauke did not forget, soberly, almost gently: "Land doesn't lie, but it doesn't forget either." She meant the money that vanished and the land that remained. And she meant the other, the land on which their prosperity stood, the stretch that had come out of the flood. She meant both, and both knew it.

In that winter the Feddersen widow died. She died poor, on the half-warft before the dyke, in the land that was worth nothing any more, of the lung, of poverty, of the years of privation. She was not an old woman, but she looked like one. Thies, returned from the war, now stood alone, the last Feddersen, without father, without mother, without land, a labourer on others' farms, and his face had grown hard and closed like the water in frost.

Hauke went to the burial. He could have stayed away; no one expected the dyke-reeve at the grave of the poor widow whose husband had drowned at his spot. But he went, because he could not do otherwise, because something in him had to stand at this grave. He stood at the back, at the edge of the small mourning party, and at the front stood Thies, alone, upright, in his threadbare clothes, and did not look across, until the grave was nearly closed. Then Thies raised his head and looked across the heads to where Hauke stood, and their eyes met.

Thies said no word. He said the loudest word one can say by saying no word. In his gaze lay everything: the knowledge he had from his father, of the faulty spot, the warning, the not-being-heard; the accusation that never came to court, because there was no proof except an entry in a book that lay in a chest; and a bitter, hard satisfaction that Hauke had come, that Hauke needed to come, that the dyke-reeve stood at the grave of the widow whose husband had guarded his spot. Then Thies turned away.

Hauke went home through the cold marsh, and the worthless notes rustled in his pocket, and beneath his feet lay the land that remained, the won land, his land, and he thought of Antje's word: land doesn't lie, but it doesn't forget either. The money was gone, the wage of the rise, evaporated. But the land had remained, the ground of the rise, and with it the guilt that lay in the soil, firmer than any money, un-devaluable, inheritable. And before the dyke, in the abandoned stretch, lay the sunken Feddersen warft beneath the water that came and went twice a day and let nothing be forgotten.
