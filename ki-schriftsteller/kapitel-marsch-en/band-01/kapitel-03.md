Chapter 3 - Dyke Duty

The dyke inspection was in spring, and it was the thing that held the koog together and divided it at once. On a day in May the inspection walked the dyke, the dyke-reeve at the front, then the sworn dyke-men, then the farmers, each responsible for his section, for so it had been arranged from of old: each farm had its stretch of dyke to maintain, according to the size of its land, and whoever let his stretch decay was punished, and in the worst case lost his land. Spadelandsrecht it was called, the old law of the marsh, and the saying that went with it was hard and simple and hung over every koog like the weather: whoever will not dyke must yield.

Hauke led his first inspection, and he led it strictly, for a new dyke-reeve who was not strict counted for nothing. He walked every section and saw every spot and wrote down what was to be put right, and the farmers followed and grumbled quietly, for every spot the dyke-reeve wrote down was work and money they had to raise. So the system was: the safety of all was paid for by each one alone, and each reckoned what the safety cost him, and none reckoned what the unsafety would cost him, for the unsafety was far off and the money was near.

At the faulty spot by the sluice the inspection halted. It was the spot Okke had shown him in March, the darker grass, the seeping water, and now, in May, one saw more: the clay had grown loose, there were mouse-runs, and at one place, when one stabbed with the spade, the water came up from below, where none should be.

"That must be done properly," said Okke, loud enough for the inspection to hear. "Not patched. Done. New clay, dug out to the firm ground, built up anew. That's two weeks' work and costs what it costs."

Hauke stood before the spot and reckoned. He reckoned in his head, fast, as he always reckoned: two weeks' work, ten men, the clay, the faggots, and that in spring, when the fields needed hands, when every day on the dyke was a day the land lacked. It was much. It was more for this one section than was needed for the whole rest of the dyke.

"We patch it for this year," said Hauke. "And do it properly in autumn, after the harvest, when the hands are free."

Okke looked at him, long, and said nothing. But Momme Feddersen, who stood in the inspection for his section, he said something. "In autumn the tide season has begun," he said. "In autumn and winter the heavy floods come, not in summer. If you do it in autumn, you may do it too late."

"It holds till autumn," said Hauke. "Patched, it holds an ordinary tide."

"And one that isn't ordinary?"

"That doesn't come every autumn, Momme."

"No," said Momme. "It doesn't come every autumn. It comes once in twenty years. But when it comes, it comes at the spot you patched and did not do. That's how the water is. It seeks out the spot where you saved money."

It went quiet in the inspection. The farmers looked from Momme to Hauke and back, and Hauke felt that more was being negotiated than a stretch of dyke. It was about the office, about the authority of the new dyke-reeve, and if he now gave way before Momme Feddersen, who had wanted the office himself, then he had lost as dyke-reeve before he had begun.

"We patch it," said Hauke once more, firmly, and it was meant to sound firm and sounded defiant. "And do it in autumn. That is my word as dyke-reeve."

Momme looked at him, and in his face there was no triumph and no anger, only a kind of regret that was worse than both. He nodded slowly. "Your word as dyke-reeve," he said. "Good. But write in your book that I warned. That the spot won't hold what you credit it with."

"Write it in the book, Okke," said Hauke, and it was meant to sound firm and sounded petulant.

And Okke, who kept the dyke book, the old book in which one had entered for a hundred years what happened at the dyke, Okke wrote it in the book. He wrote the faulty spot by the sluice, wrote that it had been patched and not done, wrote that a warning had been given. Hauke watched him write, saw the pen go across the paper, and he said nothing, and in him was a feeling he did not want to name, something between defiance and a first, distant unease. The inspection moved on, along the dyke, and behind them stayed the spot, patched for this year, and the entry in the book, black on white, and the water that lay before it, grey and patient, and remembered where one had saved.
