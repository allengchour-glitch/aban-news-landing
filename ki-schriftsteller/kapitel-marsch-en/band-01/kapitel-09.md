Chapter 9 - The Boy at His Hand

Bo was born two years after the flood, in 1908, in a mild spring, and at first Hauke saw in the child only what every father sees in his firstborn: the continuation, the heir, the one for whom one worked. Only later, as the boy grew, did he see other things, and it frightened him.

Bo grew up on the warft, in the shadow of the dyke, and like all marsh children he learned early to read the water. Hauke took him along on the dyke, as Okke had once taken him, and showed him what a Carstens had to know: where the dyke was strong and where tired, how to read the grass, how to read the gauge at the sluice. The boy learned fast, faster than others, and he learned with a seriousness, a doggedness, that was not childlike. Bo wanted to do everything right, wanted to be the best, and Hauke recognised the ambition, because it was his own, the same hunger for standing, the same need to be something in this flat, hard land.

And Hauke feared him. He feared the ambition in his own son, because he knew where it could lead: to the faulty spot one does not do because it is expensive; to the sensible thrift that is no longer thrift but a gamble with the lives of others. He saw it sprouting in the boy and could say nothing against it, for the only thing he would have had to say — beware where your ambition drives you, I know it, I followed it, and a man died of it — that he could not say, never.

Thies Feddersen, Momme's son, was a few years older than Bo. After the flood the Feddersens were impoverished; the land before the dyke was worthless, the warft destroyed, the widow scraped by on the little that remained. And when Thies was old enough, he had to go to work, and there was hardly work for an impoverished boy except at the larger farms, and the largest farm nearby was the Carstenses'. So it came about that Thies Feddersen started as a herd-boy at Hauke's, at the man at whose faulty spot his father had drowned.

It was a humiliation no one spoke of and all saw. The son of the drowned Momme herded the cattle of the dyke-reeve who had not had the spot done. Hauke gave him good wages, more than usual, and it helped nothing, made it worse if anything, for everyone in the koog understood what these good wages were: an atonement that did not speak its name, a guilt-money that bore no name. Thies took the wages, because his mother needed them, and he took them with a face that betrayed nothing, a closed, hard boy's face, and Hauke could not look him in the eye.

But the two boys, Bo and Thies, cared nothing for what lay between the fathers. They scuffled and played, as boys do, herded the cattle together, built dams in the ditches, raced each other across the dyke. For them the old story was nothing, a matter of the grown-ups, a silence whose ground they did not know. Sometimes Hauke saw them sitting together on the dyke, his own son and the dead man's son, two small figures against the wide sky, and something contracted in him, and he turned away.

"Leave them," said Antje, when he once spoke of not knowing whether it was right that Bo played with the Feddersen boy. "You won't get the old story out of the children by separating them. Perhaps it's good that they're together. Perhaps it heals, a generation on, what we broke, if only we let it."

"And if it doesn't heal?" said Hauke. "If it only sleeps and wakes later?"

Antje had no answer to that, and neither did Hauke. They let the boys play, and they grew up, the one to be heir of a farm that stood on a guilt, the other to be a hand on that very farm, and neither of them knew it, and over them stood the high sky of the marsh, beneath which everything was visible and nothing was said.
