Chapter 6 - The Breach

It happened in the third hour after midnight, when the tide reached its peak, and it happened as Momme had foretold: at the spot by the sluice, not somewhere else.

Hauke was at his own section, a stretch further on, and he heard it sooner than he saw it. It was a sound he never forgot, though it nearly drowned in the storm: a sagging, a dull giving-way of the earth, and then the water, which no longer pressed but streamed, with a roar that was different from the roar of the storm, deeper, greedier. The dyke had given way. The mountain had broken.

Hauke knew at once where. He knew it without looking, with the certainty of one who knew the spot, who had been warned, who had seen the entry in the book. The spot by the sluice. Momme's spot. He let the spade fall and ran, against the storm, along the dyke crown, and his heart beat in his throat, and he prayed, without knowing he prayed, that it was not so bad, that Momme had drawn back in time, that no one —

He came to the spot. In the light of the few lanterns still burning he saw the breach: the dyke was torn away over twenty metres, and through the gap the water shot into the koog, a black, roaring stream that swept everything with it. And in the water, in the breach, in the whirling, roaring dark, he saw Momme for a moment. He saw him struggle, saw the arms, saw the face that looked up to him, and then the water tore him away, into the koog, into the darkness, and Hauke saw him no more.

"Momme!" Hauke cried it into the storm, and the storm took it from his mouth. He wanted to go down, into the breach, into the water, but hands held him fast, Okke's hands, other hands, for whoever climbed into that breach was lost, the water was too strong, swept everything with it, and a dead dyke-reeve saved no one.

"You can't!" Okke cried into his ear. "You can't go after him! The water takes you too!"

They held him, and Hauke stood on the breaking dyke and watched the water stream into his koog, into the land he was to have secured, through the spot he had not had done. Below, in the koog, the lights went out, one farm after another, as the water reached them; one heard the cattle bellow, heard cries, far off, in the storm. The warften became islands, one after another, and the water rose in the land, where no water should be, and Hauke stood and watched it and could do nothing, nothing more, for what should have been done one would have had to do in autumn, or in spring, or on the day the inspection halted before the spot and Okke said it must be done, not patched.

The night was long. They saved what they could, pulled people from roofs and from the rising water, drove cattle onto the warften, hauled bags into the breach that the water tore out again. The Carstens warft held; it lay high and far from the breach, and Antje and the maids brought the cattle into the farmhouse and stoked fires and cooked for those who arrived soaked and half-frozen. But the lower warften, nearer the breach, filled up, and the Feddersen warft, the smallest, the poorest, lay in the midst of the new water, and by morning of it one saw only the roof.

When the first grey light crept over the flooded land, Hauke stood at the edge of the breach, through which the water now streamed more slowly, because the level inside the koog and outside had equalised. They had not found Momme. They would find him later, in the silt, a stretch inland, where the water had carried him. Hauke stood and looked over the land that had become a sea, grey water to the horizon, out of which the warften rose like the backs of drowning animals, and he knew, with a clarity that made him colder than the storm, why the spot had broken, and who had guarded it, and that the two were connected, and that he would never be able to make it not have happened, all his life.

Okke stepped beside him. The old man said nothing for a long time. Then he said, quietly, so only Hauke heard: "The book, Hauke. It's in there. That the spot wasn't done. That a warning was given."

Hauke did not answer. He looked over the water, and in him formed, before he even thought it, the first line of what he would do, what he would not do, what he would say and not say. And Okke, who had turned his leathery face into the grey, waited to see if the dyke-reeve would say something, and the dyke-reeve said nothing, and in that silence, on the morning after the flood, over the drowned land, the other thing began, the thing that would last longer than any flood.
