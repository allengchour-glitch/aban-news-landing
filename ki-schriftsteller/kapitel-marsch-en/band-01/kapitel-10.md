Chapter 10 - War and Dyke

When the war came, in the summer of 1914, it drew the young men out of the koog, as it drew them everywhere, and the flat land grew emptier still than it already was. The farms stayed with the old, the women, the half-grown, and the dyke, which did not ask whether it was war or peace, the dyke had to be held all the same, with half a crew.

Hauke was too old for the front, forty-six, and he was dyke-reeve, and a dyke-reeve was needed where he was. So he stayed and held the dyke, and he held it with the hands that were left: with old men who remembered floods the young had only heard of, with women who dug as well as men, with boys like Bo and Thies who had to lend a hand earlier than their age foresaw. Every repair now became a question of sacrifice: whoever dug at the dyke was missing in the field, and there were too few hands for both.

Thies Feddersen went to the war. He had grown old enough, and he went half voluntarily, half out of defiance, as his father had once half out of defiance warned. Perhaps it was the prospect of escaping the koog, the place where he was the drowned man's son, the hand on the farm that had his father on its conscience. Perhaps it was only what drove all young men that summer, the enthusiasm one no longer understood afterward. Hauke watched him go, with the others, to the station in the county town, and he thought of Momme, who now saw even his only son go off to a war, the war of the gentlemen, for whom a Feddersen counted as little as a Carstens, and yet the Carstens son Bo, too young for the front, counted more in this world than the Feddersen son, who was old enough to die.

Hunger came with the war, slowly, the way the water came at flood, first imperceptibly, then everywhere. The marsh nourished, it gave corn and cattle, but the state took, requisitioned, and what remained was not enough. Antje ran the economy of scarcity, as she ran everything, with iron precision, and Hauke saw her grow thinner and the children too, and he, as dyke-reeve, had a little more influence, a little more possibility, and he used it, as he always used it: not only for himself.

For Hauke gave. He had never quite stopped giving since the flood, secretly, to the widows, to the poor, to the families of those the war took. And more than to all, he gave, through go-betweens, to the Feddersen widow, whose son now stood in the field, who was alone on her half-warft before the dyke. It did not help him, he knew that; it did not make the breach un-happen, did not give Momme back his life. It was an atonement that calmed only him, not the one to whom the wrong had been done. But he did it all the same, because he could not leave it, because a man who carries such a guilt must do something, even if it is the wrong thing, the insufficient thing.

In that war year a smaller storm tide came, in autumn, nothing against 1906, but enough to call the koog to dyke watch. And the new dyke held. The spot by the sluice, which was now done properly — that Hauke had done first of all after the flood, higher and stronger than everything — that held without difficulty. Hauke stood that night at this very spot, at the spot where Momme had drowned, and the new, strong dyke held the water, and he felt no relief, only a cold bitterness: that he now did, thoroughly and without hesitation, what he had then saved on, and that this Now helped a dead man not at all. He inspected the dyke from then on with the fear of the one who knows, over-thoroughly, left no spot weak any more, spent no money out of thrift, and the koog praised its conscientious dyke-reeve, and no one knew that this conscientiousness had a dead man at its root.
