Chapter 9 - No One Asks

Henryk's death made no waves. A dead forced labourer counted for nothing in those years; he was recorded, briefly, in a list, died in the dyke service, and buried, without a cross, without a name, at the edge of the cemetery, where one buried the foreigners. No one asked how he had come to the dangerous spot, no one asked whether it could have been otherwise. It was war, and in war people died, and one forced labourer more or less did not stand out.

Bo told himself, the way such things let themselves be told: it had been the storm, the water, the breaker, not him. He had only divided, the way one had to divide, with too few men, in a heavy night. Henryk had known the spot. It had been a sensible assignment. So Bo told himself, and it helped him by day, when he worked, and it did not help him at night, when he lay awake and heard the water and saw the boy before him who had pulled him from the ice.

Grete knew what had happened. She had heard the assignment, she had seen Bo set Henryk at the spot, and she had looked at Bo when they found Henryk, and read everything in his face. She said nothing. What should she have said. But between her and Bo there now grew a second silence, beside the inherited one, of which she knew nothing — a silence about Henryk, that laid itself onto the silence about the old flood, that lay in the chest, in the dyke book, unread. Two silences, one upon the other, the way the silt lays itself layer upon layer over the land.

Thies Feddersen, the old labourer, looked at Bo in those days, the way he had once looked at Hauke. Thies knew nothing exact — he had not heard the assignment, did not know the circumstances — but he saw Bo's face, the grey, closed face after the flood, and he recognised something in it, a resemblance that made him shiver. "Your family," he said once, in passing, while clearing the dyke, without looking at Bo, "your family has its own relation to the water. It never takes you. It always takes the one beside you." And he walked on, and Bo stayed standing with the spade in his hand, and the sentence struck him like a blow, because it was true and Thies could not know how true.

Bo did not quite understand what Thies meant, and yet he sensed it. It always takes the one beside you. Momme, back then. Henryk, now. And between them the Carstenses, who survived, who gained, who rose. Bo leafed that night again in the dyke book, which lay in the chest, sought the old entry of 1906, read it once more — the faulty spot, patched and not done, the dyke-reeve warned —, and this time he came nearer the truth, sensed the connection, sensed that his father had done or left undone something that had to do with Momme's death. But he did not think it to the end. He was tired, and the war was not yet over, and he had enough to carry himself. He shut the book and laid it back, and that was his part of the silence: that he held the truth twice in his hand and twice laid it back.
