Chapter 2 - Local Farm Leader

The office of local farm leader was small, measured against what it triggered in Bo. He administered the koog's delivery quota, kept lists of who had to deliver how much, corn, cattle, milk, and he decided, within the limits set him, who was favoured and who was pressed. It was power over the neighbours, and Bo, who had wanted all his life to be something, relished it without admitting it to himself.

He called it order. When a smallholder did not meet the quota, Bo pressed him, hard, in the name of the regulation, and told himself he was only doing his duty; the regulation was not his, he only carried it out. It was the same self-deception with which his father had not done the faulty spot — I do only the sensible, the necessary thing — and Bo repeated it, without knowing he repeated it, for he did not know the father's story.

The hardness struck the weakest first, as it always strikes the weakest first. An old cottager who could not raise the quota because he was too ill to work; a widow whose farm was too small. Bo carried out what was prescribed, and rarely softened it, for mercy was not foreseen and made one suspect. Grete saw it and said once, cautiously: "Old Petersen can't deliver, Bo. He's ill. You know it." And Bo said: "The quota is the quota. If I turn a blind eye for one, they all come." It sounded sensible, and it was hard, and Grete was silent, for now.

Thies Feddersen came under pressure. As a labourer, landless, he was suspect anyway in a time that declared the soil sacred and mistrusted the soil-less. There were lists with names like Thies's on them, people without a farm, without firm ties, and Bo could have protected Thies, with a word, with the surety of the employer. He did the most necessary, no more: certified that Thies was a good worker, indispensable at the dyke. It was enough to keep Thies, and it was as little as it had to be, coldly reckoned, without warmth.

Grete saw the transformation of her husband, slowly, over the months. Bo was not cruel, not by nature; he was ambitious, and the ambition made him compliant, made him one who carried out without asking, who looked away where looking hurt. It was the transformation of many in that time, no special one, and precisely that made it bad: that it was so ordinary, so easy, that a decent man like Bo, a capable farmer, a good dyke-reeve, grew into it, step by step, without ever taking the one great step that would have frightened him.

At the dyke inspection that year Bo showed himself as what he was: a specialist who knew the dyke and held it well. But at one spot, where an expensive, thorough repair would have been needed, he hesitated, reckoned, deferred — and Grete, who walked along, saw it and thought of something she could not name, a family resemblance that went deeper than the features of the face. Bo shunned the expensive precaution, as his father had shunned it, and neither of them knew it was the same thing, and neither knew what it had cost the last time.
