Chapter 8 - The February Flood

It came in February 1943, a heavy storm tide, at northwest storm and high tide, and the whole koog was called to dyke watch, as ever, every man to his section. But there were few men, for the war had taken them, and so was called up whoever was there: old men, half-grown boys — and the forced labourers, who, rightless as they were, could be set at the most dangerous spots without anyone asking.

Bo divided the watches. It was his duty as dyke-reeve to distribute them, and there was a spot that was softened, dangerous, where the water pressed, a spot that needed an experienced, alert man — or one whom one could burn through, if it went wrong. And Bo, under the pressure of the night, with too few hands, before the choice of setting a farmer or a forced labourer at the spot, said: "Henryk. You take the spot at the weir. You know it."

It was true, Henryk knew the spot. And it was a pretext, for the true reckoning was a different one: if the spot held, it was good; if it did not hold, it was a forced labourer, no farmer, no son of the koog. Bo did not think it so clearly. He would never have dared to think it so clearly. But beneath the words, there where a man decides before he knows he has decided, the reckoning was already made, the same reckoning his father had made when he set Momme at the faulty spot.

Bo could have done it otherwise. He could have set Henryk at a safe spot, the one who had saved his life, to whom he owed everything. He could have set himself at the dangerous spot. He did not. He set Henryk at the spot at the weir, and Henryk went, through the storm, without contradiction, for a forced labourer had no contradiction, and Bo watched him go, and in him was a pull he knew and would not name.

In the night, at the peak of the flood, the spot at the weir gave way. Not wholly, not to a breach as in 1906; the dyke held, barely, because one stacked sandbags in time. But the water took what stood at the spot, in a moment when a breaker washed over the dyke, and that was Henryk. They found him in the morning, a stretch inland, in the silt, where the water had carried him, as it had once carried Momme.

Bo stood in the morning at the spot, and it was the same knowledge his father had carried that he now carried: he had saved his own, a farmer, a son of the koog, by setting another at the dangerous spot, one who had saved his life, to whom he owed everything. He could have spared him. He had not done it. And no one would ever call him to account for it, for it had only been a forced labourer, a war-dead among millions, died in the dyke service, no one's guilt, an act of God, the war.

Bo was sick at the dyke, bent over, retched up the bile, as his father had been sick, forty years earlier, on the same coast, after a different flood. The body knew what the head talked itself out of. The apple had not fallen far. The Carstenses saved themselves and let the other fall, and the water had written the story a second time, almost word for word, and Bo knelt in the silt beside the dead boy who had pulled him from the ice, and knew that he now carried what his father had carried, unnamed, unprovable, all his life.
