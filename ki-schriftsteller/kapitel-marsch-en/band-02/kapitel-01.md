Chapter 1 - The Heir

Bo Carstens led the farm and the dyke-reeve's office with a competence no one denied him, and with a hardness some indulged him and none named aloud. He was in his mid-twenties when he took over both, and he bore it as if born to it, which in a sense he was: the fifth Carstens on the warft, heir to a farm that under the hereditary farm law was now indivisible and untouchable, grown over five generations, wrested from the sea piece by piece.

He knew the dyke like his own body, every metre, every spot — except for the one secret that lay in the chest and that he had overlooked. He walked the inspections strictly, let no weakness through, did not save at the dyke; that his father had impressed on him, with his last clear words: never save at the dyke. Bo kept to it, without knowing why it had been so important to the father, and it made him a good dyke-reeve, whose conscientiousness, like the father's, had a root he did not know.

Grete ran the farm economy, and she ran it well, clear and incorruptible. She had married Bo out of reason and with affection, and she had quickly recognised what he was: a capable, ambitious man who set the farm above all and lost himself in it. She had also recognised what she could not change in him, and she had learned to live with it, as women in the marsh learned to live with what they could not change.

In the stable, in the garden, at the sluice-drain, life went its course, the old course of the marsh, which had changed little in five generations: the cattle, the corn, the tide, the dyke inspection in spring, the tide season in autumn and winter. But over this old course now lay the new time, the flags, the slogans, the quota, the lists, and Bo was part of it, local farm leader, one who joined in, who carried out, who told himself it was only duty for farm and family.

Thies Feddersen was still on the farm, the last Feddersen, now a man in his forties, landless, a labourer, silent. He worked for Bo, as he had worked for Hauke, and between him and Bo lay the same old silence that had lain between him and Hauke, only the ground was even more unknown to Bo than to himself. Bo saw in Thies only the capable, closed worker who handled the dyke well, better almost than many a farmer; he did not see, or did not want to see, what this man carried in himself, the knowledge of the father who drowned at the spot, the bitterness of forty years.

On the horizon stood the new time, and Bo told himself the joining-in was only duty. He stood on the dyke, that spring of 1934, and looked over his land, and he was content, as far as a man like Bo could be content, who always wanted more. He did not know that he stood on a guilt, and he did not know that the time he was growing into would set him before the same choice his father had stood before: to save his own at the cost of another, and to call the wrong thing the necessary one.
