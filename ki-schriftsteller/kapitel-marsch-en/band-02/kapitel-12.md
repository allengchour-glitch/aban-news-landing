Chapter 12 - Rebuilding

The postwar years came, and the marsh ordered itself anew, slowly, laboriously. The hunger stayed a while yet, the scarcity, the barter, but the land nourished, as it had always nourished, and that was the advantage of the marsh over the bombed cities: whoever had land had bread.

Bo lost the dyke-reeve's office; the ban forbade him the public office, and a man with his past could not be dyke-reeve in the new time. He stayed a farmer, worked the farm, and the loss of the office humiliated and freed him at once, in a strange way. For without the office, without the list, without the small power, Bo was again nearer to what he originally was: a farmer, a man who worked with his hands, and in the shared work at the dyke, at the rebuilding, he found something of the old comradeship he had lost as an office-holder.

Democracy came into the koog, new dyke associations, elected representatives, a say. It was unaccustomed, after the years of command, and many an old man grumbled, but it prevailed, and Bo, who was no longer allowed an office, watched from outside how the koog ordered itself anew, freer, louder, more contentious.

Grete held the family together, through the scarcity, through the humiliation, as she had held it through the war. The refugee family stayed a while, then moved on, into the west, where there was work, and left gratitude behind and a gap. The children grew, into a new, more open time, a time that was other than the one Bo had grown up in, and Bo saw it with a mixture of relief and strangeness.

Thies Feddersen worked a few more years, then he grew too old. He died in the early fifties, the last Feddersen of his generation, alone, without land, without a farm, the son of the man who drowned at the faulty spot, who had worked forty years on the farms of the Carstenses and knew their guilt and had never been able to prove it. Bo went to his burial, as Hauke had once gone to the burial of the Feddersen widow, and stood at the back, and it was as if that too repeated itself, as if history were a tidal flow that always casts the same images onto the shore.

But Thies had descendants. A daughter who had moved away, and through her the line of the Feddersens would go on, thin, distant, but not extinguished, and one distant day a Feddersen would come back to the coast, as a Carstens would come back, and the two lines, which had never greeted each other, would meet again at the old place, at the dyke, at the water, at the spot where long ago a man drowned.

Bo, aged, prematurely aged by the war and by what he carried, stayed on the farm, a simple farmer now, and the dyke book stayed in the chest, with the entry he had read twice and not quite understood and yet sensed. And over the marsh life went on, poorer and freer at once, and before the dyke lay the old foreland under water, with the sunken Feddersen warft, and the water came and went, twice a day, patient, and remembered what the people forgot.
