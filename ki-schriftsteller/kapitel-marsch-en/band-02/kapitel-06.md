Chapter 6 - Henryk

Henryk Wójcik came in the spring of 1941, with a transport from the east, and was assigned to the Carstens farm, under Bo. He was nineteen, a slight boy with light eyes and hands that were deft, a farmer's son from a village near Thorn, whom they had dragged off his field, into a marsh he had never heard of, into a language he did not understand, into a labour meant to use him up.

On his chest he wore a sign, a sewn-on piece of cloth that marked him as what he was in the language of the time: a man of the second class, a tool. He was not allowed to eat at the family table, not into their house, not into the church. He slept in a chamber over the stable, got less and worse food, and more work, harder. He was rightless, and all knew it, and most behaved accordingly.

Bo was Henryk's master. So the situation was, soberly: a man who as a boy had scuffled with the Feddersen boy, who was good with Grete and carried his children on his shoulders, was now set over another human being like over an animal, with power over his life, and the time demanded that he exercise this power like over an animal.

In the first weeks Bo lowered his gaze. It was his method. He assigned Henryk where there was work, drove him where the quota demanded, and did not look at him while doing it, for to look at him would have meant to see him as a human being, and that Bo could not bear. So he looked away, treated the tool as a tool.

But Henryk would not let himself be quite overlooked. He was a good worker, that was the first thing that penetrated Bo's lowered gaze: the boy could handle the land, had a feel for the dyke, for the water, learned quickly what was to be done. And he bore a dignity, despite the rags, despite the sign, an uprightness that shone through everything, and precisely it made it hard for Bo to see him as an animal, for animals have no dignity.

Grete passed Henryk food over the stable door. She did it secretly, in the dusk, a piece of bread, a bowl of what was left, and Henryk took it with a look that said more than words. It was dangerous; whoever supplied forced labourers beyond the measure was punished. Grete did it all the same, and she said nothing of it to Bo, because she knew it would burden him, him, the local farm leader, the man with the office.

Bo noticed it all the same. The bread that was missing. Grete's way in the dusk. Her silence about it. And he said nothing, and in his silence lay for the first time not only looking-away, but also something else: a quiet tolerance, almost a gratitude, that his wife did what he did not dare, that in his house someone did the right thing, even if it was not he. It was little. But it was a first crack in his looking-away, a first light, and it came, the way the good often comes, not through him, but through the woman at his side.
