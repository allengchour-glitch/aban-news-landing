Chapter 4 - The Quota

With the rearmament came the pressure on the land. The Reich wanted to be self-sufficient, independent of imports, and that meant: the farmers had to deliver, more and more, and the quota that Bo had to administer rose year by year. It was a screw that slowly tightened, and Bo tightened with it, for that was his office.

He collected the quota, even from the poorest, even where it hurt. Old Petersen did not deliver enough, and Bo pressed him, and Petersen finally delivered by giving up his seed corn, and Bo knew that meant the next shortage, and collected it all the same, because the quota was the quota. He told himself he had no choice; the quota came from above, he only carried it out. It was true and was a lie, both at once, as always.

The years of rearmament were good years for Bo's own farm. Prices rose, demand was there, and a hereditary farm, protected and favoured, prospered. A modest prosperity returned, greater than under old Hauke: new implements, a second horse, a further child. Bo rose, and the rise confirmed to him that he was doing right, that joining-in and being-capable paid off, and he did not ask at whose cost the rise came, as little as his father had asked.

The price was the looking-away. People disappeared, here too, in the wide, flat marsh, where one thought oneself far from everything. A neighbour who had talked too loud. A travelling worker who was not the right sort. One lowered the gaze, and Bo lowered it with them, and told himself it was none of his affair, he was a farmer and dyke-reeve, no politician, and what those up there did was not his business. It was the attitude of most, and it was complicity, and the transition between the two was so fluid that none could name the point at which the one became the other.

Grete held her distance, inwardly. She did not go to the meetings when she could avoid it, did not sing the songs, hung out the flag only when she had to. She held the family together and the children out of the worst, as well as she could, and she saw her husband change and said little, for what should she have said, against the time, against the office, against Bo's conviction that he was only doing his duty.

Once, in those years, on a Sunday, they sat five together in the garden behind the warft, Bo, Grete, the children, and it was a picture of peace: the sun, the growing cabbage, the children playing in the grass. It was a real peace, not a pretended one, an hour of happiness. But over the garden, behind the dyke, lay the water that came and went twice a day, and over the land lay the time that ran toward a war, and Bo sat in his happiness and did not know it was on loan, and that the reckoning would come, as it always came, and that the children who now played in the grass would one day have to pay for what the fathers did and looked away from.
