Chapter 7 - On the Ice

It happened in the ice winter of 1942, at the sluice-drain, where the water between koog and outer dyke was regulated. Bo had gone out to check the sluice, for the frost was straining the gates, and the ice stood thick on the drain, and Bo stepped onto it to free an iced-up spot at the weir, and the ice, which looked firm, broke.

He sank in, into the ice-cold water, which lamed his limbs at once, and the suck pulled him under the ice, toward the sluice, and for a moment there was the cold clarity of death, the knowledge that in such water one had minutes, no more, that the cold killed faster than the drowning.

Henryk was nearby, at the dyke, and he saw it, and he acted without hesitating. He lay flat on the ice, to spread his weight, and pushed himself forward, to the break, and held Bo out a dyke-batten, which Bo grasped in lying, and pulled, with a strength one would not have credited the slight body, pulled Bo out of the hole, across the ice, onto land. It went quickly and without words, and then they both lay on the firm ground, Bo soaked and blue with cold, and Henryk beside him, gasping.

It was the same scene that had once bound Hauke and Momme, nearly forty years earlier, on the same coast, in the same water: a man who pulled another out of the water. The water repeated itself, the way it always repeated itself, and put to Bo the same question it had once put to his father: what do you owe the one who saved your life?

Bo looked at Henryk, for the first time really looked at him, the boy who had just saved him, him, the master, the man of the system that had deported Henryk. Henryk could have watched the ice pull Bo under; no one would have held it against him, no one could have proved it against him. Instead he had saved him, out of a reflex of humanity that sat deeper than all circumstances, and in this reflex lay an accusation sharper than any reproach: Henryk had shown himself a human being in the moment when Bo treated him as a tool.

"Dziękuję," said Bo, the one Polish word he had picked up, thank you, and it was the first time he addressed Henryk like a human being.

Henryk only nodded, curtly, and helped Bo up, for Bo had to get into the warmth, quickly, before the cold took him after all. In the weeks that followed Bo did what he could, which was little and dangerous. He assigned Henryk the lighter work where it was possible. He slipped him bread, secretly, as Grete did, the Bo-bread to the Grete-bread. He turned a blind eye to small offences. It was a requital in small things, an attempt to pay off the debt of his life, and it was as insufficient as Hauke's charity once was, a means of living with one's own complicity, more than a real making-good.

Grete saw the change and was glad and worried at once. "Be careful," she said. "They see everything. If they notice you're sparing him, it's worse for him and for you too." And then, more quietly: "But if you help him, then help him properly. Half helps no one. Half is only for your conscience." And Bo knew she was right, as she was always right, and he feared the day when the half would no longer suffice, when he would have to choose, wholly, as his father had once had to choose.
