Chapter 5 - War on the Coast

The war came in 1939, and this time no one marched off with flags and music, at least not in the marsh, where the old still remembered the last one, the names the pastor had read from the pulpit. They were silent when the news came, a silence that knew more than any cheering.

The young men went off, from the koog as from everywhere, and the flat land grew empty, emptier still than in the last war. Bo stayed; he was a farmer and dyke-reeve and local farm leader, indispensable, needed where he was. But the work remained, and the hands were lacking: the dyke had to be held, the land tilled, the quota delivered, and all with fewer people, with old men, women, children.

And the quota grew. The war devoured, and the land had to nourish, more and more, and Bo collected it, as he had always collected it, with the hardness of one who tells himself he has no choice. Every repair at the dyke became a question of sacrifice, every sack of corn a duty. Bo manoeuvred, held farm and dyke and office together, and it wore on him, and he grew harder at it, more closed, a man who only functioned.

Then the first foreigners came onto the farms. Prisoners of war first, then the deported from the occupied lands, forced labourers, as one euphemistically called them, as if it were a kind of work and not a kind of slavery. They were assigned to the farms, because the men were lacking, and the Carstens farm too got assigned what was due to it by the size of the holding: hands that were not allowed to ask whether they wanted to.

Bo saw the first come, and he lowered his gaze, as he had learned to lower his gaze. They were people, emaciated, in rags, marked, and Bo treated them as what the system made of them: as tools, as labour, rightless. He told himself it was the war, not him; he had not deported them, he only deployed them, as was prescribed. It was the old self-deception, and it carried Bo, as it had always carried him.

Grete saw the foreigners differently. She saw people, and she saw how they were treated, and something in her rose up, quietly, helplessly. She said nothing before Bo, who had the office and the regulation, but she began, in secret, to do what she could: a piece of bread, a warm soup, secretly, in the dusk, passed to the assigned workers. It was little, and it was dangerous, for whoever helped the forced labourers beyond the prescribed made himself suspect. Grete did it all the same, without ado, as she did everything that had to be done.

And over the marsh lay the war, far off still, at fronts from which only the news came, and the dyke had to be held, and the quota delivered, and the foreigners worked and were silent, and Bo looked away, and Grete looked, and between them grew, unsaid, a difference that would soon become the question before which a human being must decide.
