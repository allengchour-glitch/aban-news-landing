Chapter 16 - What the Land Holds

Bo grew old on the farm, prematurely aged by all he carried, and in his last years he often went to the dyke, to the sluice, where Hauke had stood, and looked over the land and over the water that lay before the dyke, over the old foreland with the sunken warft.

Henryk's death sat in him as 1906 had sat in Hauke, and with the years it grew not lighter but heavier, for an old man has time to remember, and the forgetting that work grants the younger, age grants no more. He saw the boy before him who had pulled him from the ice, and he saw how he set him at the spot at the weir, and no year made that better.

Once, in those last years, when Anke's daughter was visiting again, the curious child, already bigger now, almost a young woman, Bo sat with her at the dyke, and he began to tell. "There was one," he said. "In the war. One who worked for us. Henryk was his name. He saved my life, on the ice. And I —" He faltered. The young woman looked at him, waiting, and Bo felt the sentence rise, the whole sentence this time, the truth about Henryk, about the February flood, about the spot. And behind it, older, the other truth, about the father, about Momme, about the book in the chest.

"And you what, Grandpa?" she asked.

And Bo broke off. It was too big. It was too heavy. And she was young, he told himself, one must not lay such a thing on a young person. It was a pretext, and a part of him knew it, the old Carstens pretext, rather keep the heavy than speak it. "I couldn't help him," said Bo. "The war. The water. One couldn't help many." And he took the young woman by the hand. "Come. It's getting cold."

But she did not quite let it drop, the young woman, Anke's daughter, Wiebke's mother. She remembered the name Henryk, and the broken sentence, and the word "faulty spot" that Bo had once muttered in half-sleep, and she would pass it on, without knowing it, to her own daughter, who would one day come back to the coast.

Bo died in his late years, at the window from which one saw the dyke, and Grete found him in the morning, his face finally calm. He had never said the word, neither word, neither about Henryk nor about the father. He took it with him, almost whole. Almost. For in the chest lay the dyke book, with the entry, and in the young woman lay the memory of a broken sentence, and that was enough, just enough, that it could one day break open.

The modernisation marched on, the koog grew safer, the dykes higher, the machines mightier, and the marsh forgot faster than it had forgotten before. But before the dyke still lay the calm, treacherous water over the old foreland, and it came and went, twice a day, and remembered what the people forgot, and waited, patient, for a time not yet born — a time in which the water would rise, higher than ever, and reclaim what one had taken from it, and lay bare what one had hidden in it.
