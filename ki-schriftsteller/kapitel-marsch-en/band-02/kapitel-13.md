Chapter 13 - The Son Who Stays

Bo had two children who reached adulthood: a son, Hinrich, and a daughter, Anke. And in them showed, as it always showed in the children of this family, the old division: the one who stays and preserves, the other who goes away and asks.

Hinrich was the preserver. He was diligent, dutiful, down-to-earth, a boy who wanted the farm and the dyke, who wanted to be nothing other than a farmer in the marsh, like his father, like the grandfather. He went early to the field and the dyke, learned to read the water, took on responsibility, and Bo saw in him the heir, the one who would carry on the farm, undivided, into the next generation. Hinrich did not ask after the old story; what was past was past, and the book in the chest did not interest him. He was no brooder. He was a doer, and that pleased Bo and it also disquieted him, for he recognised in it his own looking-away, his own inability to look where it hurt.

Anke was different. She was clever, headstrong, curious in a way one did not like to see in girls, and she wanted to go away: to learn, to become something, out of the marsh, out of the narrow life at the dyke. She read, she asked, she was not satisfied with the answers of the grown-ups. And she sensed, with the fine ear of those who want to leave, that over the farm lay a silence, something unspoken, an old story that no one talked about. She once asked after the flood, after the Feddersens, after the name that sometimes fell and was quickly wiped away again, and Bo evaded, as his father had evaded, and Anke remembered the evading, the way one remembers pieces of evidence.

Grete fostered Anke's headstrongness, against the resistance of the koog, which held that a girl belonged at the farm and not in the school of the city. "Let her learn," said Grete to Bo. "Nothing holds her here. And perhaps it's good if one of the family looks outward, further than to the dyke." Bo gave way, the way men in the marsh gave way when the women were right and did not say it loud, and Anke went away, to the city, to school, and came back another, in the holidays, and brought questions with her that no one in the koog asked.

So the siblings divided the inheritance, the way the inheritances in this family always divided: Hinrich, who would stay and preserve the farm and the silence, untouched; Anke, who went away and asked and saw the world from outside. And between the two, the preserver and the questioner, would be decided — a generation later, through Anke's children, through Hinrich's children — what became of the breach Hauke had made and Bo had repeated. For Anke would have a daughter who would be called Wiebke, and Wiebke would come back, not as a farmwife, but as an engineer, and she would read the water as the Carstenses had always read it, only with other eyes, and she would open the book that lay in the chest.
