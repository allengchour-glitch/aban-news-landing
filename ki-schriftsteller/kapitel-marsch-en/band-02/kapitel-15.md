Chapter 15 - The Dyke Book, Found Again

It was while clearing out the chest, on a grey day in autumn, that Bo took the dyke book in hand again, really in hand, not just a fleeting leafing. Grete had said the old chest had to be cleared out, it took up room and was full of old junk, and Bo had sat down and gone through what a hundred years of farm history had gathered: leases, parish-register entries, yellowed letters — and the dyke book, the heavy, leather-bound book that Hauke had taken over from Okke and Bo from Hauke.

This time Bo read the entry of 1906 fully, slowly, with the eyes of a man who now knew himself what it meant to leave a spot undone at the dyke, to set a person at a dangerous spot. He read: the faulty spot by the sluice. Patched and not thoroughly done. The dyke-reeve warned. Date. Okke's hand. And beneath it, what Bo had earlier overlooked, a second note, briefer, in the same hand: after the breach. A dead man at the said spot: M. Feddersen, who had the watch.

And now what Bo had known in fragments all his life fitted together. His father Hauke, dyke-reeve in 1906, had known the faulty spot and not had it done, out of thrift. The dyke broke at this spot. And at the spot Momme Feddersen died, who guarded it — Thies's father. Hence the bitterness of the Feddersens. Hence the silence. Hence the father had wanted to speak of the spot at his deathbed and had not been able to. On that, on this concealed omission, stood the farm, the won land, the whole prosperity of the Carstenses.

Bo sat long still with the book. And he saw, with a clarity that made him colder than the autumn day, the repetition: what his father had done to Momme, he had done to Henryk. A faulty, a dangerous spot. A man one set there. A dead man who saved one's own. And a silence about it that one carried on. The apple had not fallen far. Two generations, the same guilt, almost word for word, and the book in his hand proved the first and sensed the second.

Bo wrestled with the book, as his father had once wrestled with it. He could tear out the page, erase the truth, destroy the last testimony — and with it the possibility that anyone would ever learn what the farm stood on. Or he could leave it, pass it on, as Hauke had passed it on. He took the page between his fingers.

And he left it. Like his father he left it standing, the entry, the truth, and he did not quite know why, only that he could not do it, destroy the page, as little as he could speak the truth. Perhaps it was cowardice. Perhaps it was something else, something that was almost hope: that one day there would be a human being, braver than he, braver than his father, who opened the book and read and said what two generations had concealed. He laid the book back in the chest, and Grete saw him do it, saw his face, and she knew that he now carried both: the father's guilt, which he had finally understood, and his own, which so resembled the first.

"You read it," said Grete quietly. It was not a question.

"I read it," said Bo. And they said no more, for there was no more to say, and the silence that now lay between them was fuller than any word: the knowledge of two people of two guilts, the one old, the other fresh, and both enclosed in a chest, in a book, in a land that let nothing be forgotten.
