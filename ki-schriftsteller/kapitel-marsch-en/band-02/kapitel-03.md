Chapter 3 - Grete

They had married in the crisis, Bo and Grete, in the early thirties, poor and resolute, as one married then. Grete was a farmer's daughter from the neighbouring koog, with a straight gaze and a head that took nothing as given, and Bo had chosen her because she was capable and strong, and she had chosen him because he had a farm and a will, and because between them, besides reason, there was also affection.

The first years were hard and good at once, as the first years on a marsh farm often were: much work, little money, but the land bore, and the children came, and Grete made a home of the farmhouse. She was not one to complain. She took hold, stretched the little, made something of nothing, and Bo, who saw her come from the stable with earthy hands or from the sluice-drain with a basket of eels, sometimes thought he had been lucky, luckier than he deserved.

At the wedding Thies Feddersen had been there too, at the edge, as a worker of the farm, not as a guest. And when Bo once stepped out into the cool evening air, Thies stood there smoking, and they were alone for a moment, the son of the man whose father concealed the breach, and the son of the man who drowned at the breach — and Bo knew nothing of either.

"Congratulations, Carstens," said Thies. "Good woman. The Petersens are decent people."

"Thank you," said Bo, a little stiff, for he sensed that something lay between Feddersen and Carstens, an old coldness whose ground he did not know.

Thies smoked and looked at him, long. "You don't know much, do you," he said at last. "About your family. About what was. About the flood."

"What's supposed to be with the flood? That's thirty years ago."

"Ask your father," said Thies, and then it occurred to him that Hauke was dead, and something crossed his face. "Or ask the book. The dyke book. If you can read what's in it." He trod out the cigarette. "Mind yourself in the times that are coming, Carstens. Bad times are coming. And in bad times it shows who a man is. Some families," he looked at Bo, "some families have already shown once what they do when it gets tight. They look away. They save at the wrong end. Mind you don't look away where you ought to look."

Bo did not understand the barb. He heard an embittered labourer making dark hints, and he did not want to understand them, because it was his wedding day. "I don't look away," he said, almost amused. "I have nothing to look away from."

"Everyone has something to look away from," said Thies. "Sooner or later. That's just it." And he went back to his work, and Bo stayed in the evening air, and the sentence stuck in him, without his knowing why, like a stone in the shoe.

Inside, later, Bo thought no more of it. But Grete, who had seen the short conversation from afar, asked him: "What did Thies want?" And Bo said: "Nothing. Old stories. He's embittered, the man, ever since his father drowned, back then in the flood." And Grete remembered the name and the bitterness, and she remembered that there was something between the families that no one spoke of, and she was a woman who remembered things and did not forget.
