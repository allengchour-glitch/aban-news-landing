Chapter 14 - Land Under, Land Over

The fifties brought the upswing, and they brought it to the coast too, in the shape of machines. Where earlier human hands had dug the silt from the watt, stuck the groynes, thrown the clay onto the dyke, now came excavators, pumps, machines that did in days what one had earlier needed weeks and many hands for. The land reclamation, the old, patient work of generations, was mechanised, accelerated, and new koogs grew into the sea, faster than ever before.

Bo, aged, saw the machines come and felt what old men feel when the world changes: a mixture of wonder and strangeness. He had held the dyke with his hands, all his life, with spade and faggots and the strength of many men, and now a machine did it faster and with fewer people, and it was progress, and it took something from the work that Bo could not name: the comradeship perhaps, the knowledge that one depended on one another.

But the old foreland of 1906 stayed as it was: undyked, given over to the water. One could have reclaimed it, with the new machines, could have dyked it in, the deep stretch in which the sunken Feddersen warft lay. But it did not pay; the land was too deep, too salted, and there was easier land to gain elsewhere. So the foreland stayed outside, a stretch of watt and salt-meadow before the dyke, with the remains of the Feddersen warft that no one saw any more, that only the very old knew and of which the young did not even know that a farm had once stood there.

In those years Bo began to want to say something to a grandchild. Anke had married, in the west, and brought in the holidays a child along, a little girl, curious like the mother, and the child asked after everything, also after the water before the dyke, after the stretch where the watt was. "There was land there once," said Bo. "Earlier. Long ago. A farm stood there." And the child asked: "Who lived there?" And Bo said: "Feddersen. The Feddersens lived there." And he wanted to say more, wanted to tell, of the flood, of Momme, of the spot — and he broke off, as his father had broken off. "Come," he said. "It's getting cold." And took the child by the hand and went back to the warft.

The child, though, remembered the name, Feddersen, and the stretch of water where a farm had stood, and it remembered that the grandfather had wanted to say something and had not said it. It was Anke's daughter, and she was not yet called Wiebke — that would be only a generation later — but she carried the curiosity on, the not-forgetting, the asking, and through her it would go on, the thread that led from the grandfather's broken sentence to the granddaughter's opened book, across the years, patient, as the water was patient that lay before the dyke and waited.
