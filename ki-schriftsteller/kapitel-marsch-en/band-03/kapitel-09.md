Chapter 9 - The Koog Speaks

Wiebke laid both before them, in the same week: the depoldering plan with the unavoidable opening of the old dyke, in the village hall, before the whole koog, and, as part of the accompanying chronicle, the historical truth about the flood of 1906, which she and Lena had written down together, soberly, with evidence, without accusation.

The town meeting was full, for it was about land, about home, about the future of the koog, and that brought the people, as it always brought them. Wiebke explained the plan: the new dyke inland, the opening of the old one, the foreland given back to the sea and to nature conservation. She explained the necessity, the rising level, the costs, the safety. And she saw that she met resistance, the anger of those who would lose land, Jan above all, and the deeper aversion of a kind of people whose whole history was reclaimed land and for whom giving up land was like a defeat.

And then, when it came to the chronicle, to the history of the koog one wanted to publish for the depoldering, Wiebke brought the other matter to speech: that the flood of 1906 had not been only an act of God, that there had been a known faulty spot, a warning, an omission, and a dead man who died for it. She named the names, both: Momme Feddersen, who drowned, and Hauke Carstens, the dyke-reeve, who had not had the spot done. Her own great-grandfather.

The koog did not react with the outcry Lena had perhaps expected and feared. It reacted with the old reflex of the marsh: with silence, with embarrassment, with the wish not to stir up. The chairman of the dyke association spoke of a "historical matter that had to be sorted out factually". A few old people who still knew or sensed something came later to Wiebke, quietly, at the door, in the street, and said it was good it was out, their grandparents had always muttered something of the flood and the Carstenses and the Feddersens, and never had anyone spoken it. Others turned away. Some Carstens relatives, distant ones, no longer greeted Wiebke. Jan sat in the meeting with a closed face and said nothing.

It was no sensation, no scandal, and precisely that Wiebke had wanted. But it was also no redemption, no moment in which the silence broke and the koog breathed out. It was more sober, more laborious: an old truth was named, took its place in the chronicle, and life went on, a little different, a little more honest. Lena stood beside Wiebke as the meeting dissolved, and said: "I'd have thought it would feel different. Bigger." And Wiebke said: "So did I. But perhaps that's all there is. Not one great moment. Only the end of the concealing."
