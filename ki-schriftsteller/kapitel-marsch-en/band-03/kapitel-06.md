Chapter 6 - Jan at the Dyke

While Wiebke wrestled with the past, Jan fought with the present, and his present was the old dyke, which held and would not hold much longer. On a day in autumn a heavy west wind came up, and with it a raised tide, nothing catastrophic, but enough to tear open a weak spot at the old dyke — seeping water, a softening section, exactly the kind of spot a dyke-man fears.

Jan was the first there, and he called whom he could get, and a construction crew came that the water board sent, men with sandbags and gear, and among them was a Romanian worker, one of those who today did the work for which no locals could be found any more, as earlier the Poles and the Turks. They worked together, Jan and the crew, stacked bags, secured the spot, for hours, in the wind and the spraying water, and at the end the dyke held.

And it was, as it always was at the dyke: in the work, in the danger, it did not count where one came from. The Romanian worker took hold like the others, held the bag where Jan needed it, and Jan, who otherwise thought little of incomers, reached him his hand at the end and said "good work", and meant it. It was the old solidarity of the dyke, that did not ask after a passport, the same that had once bound Hauke and Momme, before the one set the other at the faulty spot. Jan lived it, unconsciously, the better part of what his family was, without knowing that his family also knew the worse part.

In the evening, exhausted, Jan sat with Wiebke, who had come to assess the weak spot — for her another proof that the old dyke was not to be held. And Wiebke, tired and full of what she had found in the records, told him. Of 1906. Of the faulty spot. Of Hauke, who was warned and did not have it repaired. Of Momme Feddersen, who drowned for it. Of the land their farm gained in the process.

Jan listened, and his face closed. "That's a hundred years ago," he said. "What's the point now. They're all dead. Leave the land in peace, Wiebke. Leave the old stories where they are."

"The land won't leave me in peace," said Wiebke. "It comes to light of itself. When we depolder, we lay bare the old foreland, the Feddersen warft, everything. The water gives back what it took, and with the land comes the history. I can't leave it in the silt, Jan. It's already half out."

"So you want to spread it about," said Jan. "Our family at the pillory. The farm, the land — everything we have. You're sawing the branch we sit on."

"I want to name it. Not spread it about. That's a difference." Wiebke looked at her cousin, the preserver, who held the land and the silence, and she saw in him Bo and Hauke, the same attitude, the same holding-on, the same looking-away. "You're like them, Jan. Like the grandfather, like the great-grandfather. Rather hold the land and keep silent than look. But the water is rising, and it takes the land from us anyway. The only question is whether we let the truth be washed out with it or whether we speak it ourselves, honestly."

Jan was silent, and between the cousins ran a crack, fine but distinct, the same crack that ran through the family for a hundred years: between the one who preserves and the one who names. And Wiebke went that evening from Jan's farm without their having come to agreement, and she knew she would have to make the decision alone, or with Lena, but not with Jan, who held the land and the silence, because he could not do otherwise.
