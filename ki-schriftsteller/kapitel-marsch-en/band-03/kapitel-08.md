Chapter 8 - What to Do with It

Wiebke and Lena met again, this time in Lena's kitchen, and they wrestled with the question that had no easy answer: what does one do with a truth that benefits no one any more and harms no one any more, because all who are concerned are dead?

Lena wanted to make it public, fully, at once. "For a hundred years my great-grandfather has counted as the one who simply had bad luck, who drowned in a natural catastrophe. And the truth is that he died because a Carstens saved at the wrong end. That belongs in the light. That's the least one owes him."

Wiebke was more cautious. "What good does it do him? He's been dead a hundred years. No scandal rehabilitates him any more."

"It does his memory good. His descendants. The truth."

"And it shames mine," said Wiebke. "My cousin Jan, who can do nothing about it. And perhaps it shames more than just us. Whoever in this marsh has come up a little, over the generations, has perhaps also saved at the wrong end sometime, looked away, taken an advantage where another lost. If we open this up, where does it stop?"

She was tempted, Wiebke felt it, tempted to let it rest. It would have been so easy. No one knew the records except her and Lena. She could lay them back, carry the plan through technically, flood the foreland, and the old story would stay where it had lain a hundred years: in the silt, under water. It was the same temptation that had bound her great-grandfather and brought her grandfather to silence, and Wiebke recognised it: it worked not through threat but through reason, through the quiet voice that says it is better for everyone to let the heavy rest. So all the Carstenses had kept silent. So the marsh kept silent about so much. Silence was cheap, and speech was dear, and Wiebke understood for the first time fully why four generations had chosen the cheap.

She chose the dear. But she chose it her way, not Lena's.

"I won't make it a scandal," she said. "No opening-up, no accusing, no pillory. Nothing gets better from that, it only breeds new grudge, and our families have had grudge long enough. But neither do I bury it. That's the third way, Lena, between the silence and the scandal: we name it. We give the truth where it belongs. There'll be a chronicle, for the depoldering, for the history of the koog — and there's to be a memorial plaque at the uncovered place, at the Feddersen warft. That's where it belongs, all of it: the flood of nineteen-hundred-six, the faulty spot, the death of your great-grandfather, with names, certified, readable. Not hidden and not blown up into a scandal. Simply true. Simply there. Whoever wants to know it can read it. No one who doesn't want to has to. But it is no longer hidden."

Lena thought a long time. "That's less than I wanted," she said at last.

"I know. But it's more than any Carstens ever admitted. And it's what I can carry without doing new harm. Momme Feddersen gets his name back, with the truth, in the chronicle, on the plaque, forever. And Hauke Carstens gets his too, beside it, with what he did. Both names, both truths. That's justice, as far as I can give it."

And Lena, after a long silence, nodded. "Good," she said. "I'll go that way with you. But we do it together, Wiebke. A Feddersen and a Carstens. So no one can say it's the revenge of the one or the whitewash of the other."

"Together," said Wiebke, and they shook hands, a Carstens and a Feddersen, for the first time in a hundred years, over the table on which the records lay, and it was no reconciliation, not yet, but it was the beginning of something larger than the enmity they had inherited, and smaller than the forgiveness neither of them had to give: a shared looking, a shared naming, the end of the silence.
