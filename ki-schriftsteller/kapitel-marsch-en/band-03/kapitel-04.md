Chapter 4 - The Uncovered Foreland

The depoldering works began in autumn, with the preliminary survey: one measured the foreland, mapped the subsoil, determined where the new dyke would run and where the old one would be opened. Wiebke directed it, and it was her work to document the terrain, every metre, and so she came out, into the old foreland, the deep stretch before the dyke that had been left to the water since 1906.

At low water, at ebb, the foreland lay bare, a wide, silty watt with channels and salt-meadows, and the survey works, the carrying-off of silt for the borings, laid bare what had long been hidden. At one spot, there where the old dyke had broken in 1906, an excavator struck masonry — old bricks, a foundation, a buried well. The remains of a farmstead that had stood here before the water came.

Wiebke stood before it, in the silt, in rubber boots, and saw the uncovered foundations, and she knew what it was before anyone confirmed it. The Feddersen warft. The farm that went under in 1906, when the dyke broke. Here Momme Feddersen had lived, the man who drowned at the faulty spot of her great-grandfather. Here, beneath the silt of a hundred years, lay the ground on which the history of her family stood.

It was a biologist on the project team who was there that day, a woman in her thirties, who mapped the salt-meadows and accompanied the renaturation, and when the foundations appeared, she came over and looked at them, long, with a face in which something worked. "That was a farm," she said. "Someone lived here once."

"The Feddersen warft," said Wiebke. "It went under, nineteen-hundred-six. In the dyke breach."

The biologist looked at her. "How do you know that?"

"From the records. I'm Carstens. Wiebke Carstens." She said the name, and she saw how it landed with the other woman, how something changed in her face, grew cooler, more watchful.

"Carstens," said the biologist slowly. "And I'm Lena Feddersen." She let the name stand, between them, in the silt, over the uncovered foundations. "Then we both know, I suppose, that this is no ordinary farm appearing there."

It was the first time the two lines met again, a Carstens and a Feddersen, at the same place, over the sunken warft, a hundred years after the flood that had separated them. And there lay a coldness between them, at once, the old coldness of the village, the not-greeting, of which neither had known the exact ground and which now, over the uncovered stones, got a ground. But beneath the coldness lay also something else, an attention, a recognition that they both knew or sensed something larger than their strangeness, and that this something here, in the silt, was just now coming to light, stone by stone, the way the water gave it up, the water one would now let rise again.
