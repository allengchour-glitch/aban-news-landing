Chapter 3 - The Records

The archive of the dyke and water board lay in a sober building in the county town, and Wiebke spent days in it, among shelves full of files that reached back into the last century. She sought the gauge records, the breach statistics, everything that supported her depoldering plan, and she was thorough, as she was always thorough, and so she read too what she had not sought.

She found the protocols of the great storm tide of 1906. The breach at the Sankt-Annen-Koog was recorded in them, at length, for it had been a heavy breach, with dead and great land-loss. She read the cause assessment: a spring tide at standing northwest, a level beyond all marks, an act of God. She read of the dead man who had lost his life in the breach, in the dyke service: a man named Momme Feddersen, who had guarded the endangered spot. Feddersen. The name that triggered the coldness in the village.

She read on, and in the board archive she found not only the official protocols but also old dyke documents the board had taken over when the dyke-reeve administration was modernised — among them transcripts from the old dyke books of the koogs. And in the transcript of the Sankt-Annen dyke book she found an entry of 1906, in the old hand of a dyke-warden named Okke Nissen: the faulty spot by the sluice, patched and not thoroughly done, the dyke-reeve warned. And beneath it, after the breach: a dead man at the said spot.

Wiebke sat quite still. A faulty spot one knew of. A warning not heeded. A breach at precisely this spot. A dead man who guarded it. That was not an act of God. That was, at least in part, an omission — a dyke-reeve who out of thrift had not had a known weakness repaired, and a man who died for it.

And the dyke-reeve of 1906? She sought it, in the board lists, and found it: dyke-reeve of the Sankt-Annen-Koog, 1901 into the twenties, was Hauke Carstens. Her great-grandfather. The man from whom the farm came that Jan sat on today, the land the family won. Hauke Carstens had not had the spot done at which Momme Feddersen drowned, and after the flood, the survey files showed, the Carstens farm had gained land where the Feddersens lost theirs — precisely that foreland that now, a hundred years later, Wiebke was to give back to the sea.

She remembered, all at once, something from her childhood: her mother, who had once told of her grandfather Bo, who had lain dying and talked confused stuff, of a "faulty spot" and of "Momme" and of a "book in the chest". Back then no one had understood it, had ascribed it to the fever. Now it fitted. The faulty spot. Momme. The book. It had been no fever-talk. It had been the truth, which a dying man had not been able to speak and yet had not quite wanted to conceal.

Wiebke sat in the sober archive, with the transcripts before her, and grasped that the depoldering she was planning would lay bare more than just the old sea-floor. It would, when she gave the foreland back to the water and the silt was carried off, perhaps lay bare the sunken Feddersen warft — the place at which her own family had buried a guilt, a hundred years old, embanked like the land itself. And she grasped that she now, as the first in four generations, had the choice that Hauke and Bo had not made: to look or to look away, to name or to conceal.
