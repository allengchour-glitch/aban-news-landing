Chapter 16 - High and Low Waters

Years later, on a clear day in early summer, a woman walked over the new dyke, and with her went a child she held by the hand and who plagued her with questions, the way children do.

The woman was Wiebke, older now, and the child was a great-nephew, or a great-niece — the families had mingled, Carstens and Feddersen, for Jan's daughter had married Lena's brother, and out of the two lines that had not greeted each other for a hundred years, one had become. The child holding Wiebke's hand carried both names in itself, without knowing it, and knew nothing of the old story, and that was the best of it: that for this child it was a matter of course, what the old had paid for with guilt and silence.

"What's that, Aunt Wiebke?" asked the child, and pointed out where the watt stretched, wide and gleaming at low water, with the channels and the flocks of birds.

"That used to be land," said Wiebke. "People lived there, long ago, and tilled fields, behind a dyke. And then it was decided to give the land back to the sea, because the water is rising and one could no longer hold it. Now it's watt. Now the birds live there."

"And the people?"

"They moved away. Or died, long ago." Wiebke pointed to the plaque at the dyke. "You see the plaque? There's a name on it. Momme Feddersen. He lived here, and he drowned in a great flood, over a hundred years ago. So that he isn't forgotten, his name is there."

The child spelled out the name, and then looked again at the watt. "Was it nice, before? With the land?"

Wiebke considered. It was a child's question, and it demanded a true answer. "It was hard," she said. "The people wrested the land from the sea, with their hands, generation by generation. And much wrong happened in the process, before. People looked away, saved at the wrong end, kept silent where they should have spoken. In our family too." She paused. "But there was something good there too. When the flood came, everyone helped everyone at the dyke, no matter who. That was called solidarity. That was the nicest thing about it, and it held, longer than the land."

"Do we hold together too?" asked the child.

"I hope so," said Wiebke. "I hope so very much."

They stood a moment and looked over the watt, the older woman and the child, and the wind went over the dyke, from the west, as it had always gone, and the water began to rise, for the tide was coming, as it came twice a day, since the world began. And Wiebke thought of all who had been here: of Hauke, who concealed, and Momme, who drowned; of Bo, who looked away, and Henryk, who died for him; of Antje and Grete and Thies and Lena and of herself, who had broken the silence. They all had been here, at this water, and they all were gone, and the land they had struggled over was now sea, and over the sea flew the birds, and on land a child held her hand and knew the story not as a wound, but would one day hear it as history, honestly, with guilt and with loyalty, and do better, perhaps, than the old.

"Come," said Wiebke, and took the child more firmly by the hand. "The water's coming. We have to get off the watt before the flood's here." And they went back, over the dyke, inland, away from the rising water, and behind them the watt filled, high and low waters, ebb and flood, the giving and taking of the sea, which had no end and no beginning, and which now, after a hundred years, concealed nothing any more, but only breathed, high water and low, over a land that no longer held the guilt fast.
