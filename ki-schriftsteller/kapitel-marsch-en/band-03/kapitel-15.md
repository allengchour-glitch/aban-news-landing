Chapter 15 - What Remains When the Land Sinks

The years that followed changed the Sankt-Annen-Koog more slowly than one had feared, and more deeply. The depoldered foreland became a blooming watt biotope, a protected area, into which the birds came in their thousands, in which the salt-meadows stood violet with sea-lavender in summer. Where for a hundred years land had been wrested from the sea, the land now gave back, and it was not poorer for it, only different, another kind of fertility, a wilder one that belonged to no one.

Wiebke still came back sometimes, on account of the work, of the plaque, sometimes on account of Lena, with whom a kind of friendship had grown, cautious, over the shared burden. She would then go out onto the new dyke, from which one overlooked the whole transformed land, and look over the watt, over the warften, over the sea, and think about what had happened.

For this she had learned, in those years: that it was not the truth that redeemed. She had brought the truth to light, named her great-grandfather's omission, connected the name of Momme Feddersen with the truth, broken the hundred-year silence. And she had half believed that would redeem something, take a burden from the family, bring a peace. But it was not so. The truth redeemed nothing. Hauke was still the one who had saved; Bo the one who repeated it; Momme and Henryk stayed dead, the guilt stayed guilt, no naming made that un-happen.

What had changed was one single thing, and Wiebke grasped only late that it was enough: one had stopped concealing. One had stopped holding the water over the land, the truth behind the dyke. For four generations the family and with it the whole koog had concealed a sunken matter, embanked, inherited; and now, with the concealed named and the land given back to the water, one could finally breathe out. Not relieved, not happy, not redeemed. Only honest.

And that, Wiebke thought, from the new dyke, over the given-back land, that was perhaps all a human being could achieve: not to expunge the guilt that could not be expunged, not to wake the dead, but only to stop hiding them. The solidarity Hauke had betrayed, when he set Momme at the faulty spot, was not dead; it had only changed its form, from the dyke watch into memory, from the struggle against the water into the shared living with it. Hauke, Momme, Bo, Henryk — they all got what was possible, the only thing Wiebke could give them: a name, no verdict. They were named, all of them, with their guilt and their loyalty, and they were no longer hidden, and that was the legacy that held when the land sank: the truth, spoken, and the breath, finally let go.
