Chapter 2 - The Cousin and the Land

Jan Carstens was a man in his forties, terse, stubborn, down-to-earth, a farmer who carried on the farm of his father Hinrich, as Hinrich had taken it from Bo. He received Wiebke politely and coolly, with the mistrust of the settled toward the incomer, even if the incomer was a Carstens.

"So you want to give up the dyke," he said, when they sat in his kitchen, over the coffee his wife had poured. It was not a question, it was a reproach.

"I don't want to give it up," said Wiebke. "I want to save what can be saved. The old dyke can't be held in the long run, Jan. The water is rising. If we do nothing, it breaks at some point, in a heavy flood, and then we lose everything, uncontrolled. If we depolder, we give up one stretch, controlled, and secure the rest behind it. That's not giving up. That's strategy."

"It's giving up." Jan set the cup down. "The stretch you want to give up, that's my foreland. My best land, out before the dyke. My forefathers wrested that from the sea, generation by generation, with their hands. And you come from the city and want to give it back to the water, with a stroke of the pen." He looked at her. "Whoever will not dyke must yield, it always was with us. Now I suppose it means: whoever can dyke must yield all the same, because the city wills it so."

Wiebke knew the saying, she had heard it from her mother, who had it from Bo. And she saw the land with Jan's eyes for a moment: generations of work, sweat, hands in the silt, all of it given back to the water. It was not incomprehensible, his anger. But she saw it also with the engineer's eyes: the foreland was deep, the dyke before it decrepit, the costs of its upkeep rising into the unaffordable, and the water rose further, unstoppable. It was not to be held, neither economically nor physically. That was no question of will. That was a question of gravity and of money.

"I understand what it means to you," she said. "But the water doesn't ask what it means to us. It rises. And we can choose whether we retreat in order or drown in disorder. We have no more choice than that."

Between the cousins it stayed at friction, and beneath the friction lay something else that Wiebke sensed and could not name: the old coldness that swung in the village when the name Carstens fell, a shadow over the family of which Jan too knew nothing exact and which was nonetheless there. Jan wanted to hold the land, not only from reason, but from something deeper, a bond to the soil that was almost religious — and Wiebke sensed that this bond itself was part of what had never let the family look, that the holding-on to the land and the concealing of an old guilt belonged together, even if she did not yet know how.

In the next days Wiebke began to research in the archive of the dyke and water board. She needed the historical gauge records, the old dyke files, the data on breaches and repairs, to underpin her plan. It was routine, preparation, number-work. She did not sense that she would in the process come upon something older than any gauge and deeper than any dyke: upon the year 1906, upon a faulty spot, upon a dead man and a name that was her own.
