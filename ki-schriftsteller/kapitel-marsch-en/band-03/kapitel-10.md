Chapter 10 - The Opening of the Dyke

The following autumn the new dyke was finished, a stretch inland, higher and stronger than the old one, built to the gauges of the future, not the past. And then came the day on which the old dyke was opened.

It was no breach, no catastrophe, but the opposite: a controlled opening, planned, measured, with excavators and calculation. One dug away the old dyke at one spot, let the water flow in controlled, at a middling tide, so it did not shoot but rose, slowly, and spread over the old foreland, the deep stretch that for a hundred years had been wrested from the water or left to the water.

Wiebke directed it, as an engineer, and she experienced it at once as a descendant, and the two would not be separated. She stood at the new dyke and saw the water flow in, there where in 1906 the water had broken in, only this time not in the storm, not killing, but planned, taken into service. The sea reclaimed what one had taken from it — but this time the human gave it, instead of losing it. That was the difference, and it was a great one: not the breach, but the giving-back; not the omission, but the decision.

Jan was there. He had fought against the depoldering to the last, and he had lost, and now he stood at the dyke and saw his foreland flood, the land his forebears had won, the best land of the farm. It was a hard farewell, and Wiebke saw what it cost him, and she said nothing, for there was nothing to say that would have made it easier. Jan watched the water as it rose, over the ditches, over the old field boundaries, over the spot where the Feddersen warft lay, and his face was stony, and then, after a long time, he said, quietly, almost to himself: "My grandfather always said the land doesn't forget. Now it forgets after all. It goes under water, and in twenty years no one knows there was land here."

"It doesn't go under, Jan," said Wiebke. "It becomes watt. It becomes salt-meadow. It lives on differently. And it doesn't forget — we've seen to it that it doesn't forget. It's in the chronicle. It goes on the plaque. The history stays, even when the land becomes water."

Jan did not answer. He watched the rising water until it reached the spot where the sunken warft lay, and covered it again, the stones one had uncovered, the well, the foundations — the sea reclaimed them, gently this time, and laid itself over them, smooth and grey. Out of the secret that had lain a hundred years in the silt, that one had brought to light and named, became again water and watt, tides, geology. Finished. And Wiebke thought that perhaps it was right so: that one first uncovered the truth, named it, wrote it into the chronicle — and then gave it back to the water, not as concealing, but as peace, as a land that finally went honestly under water.
