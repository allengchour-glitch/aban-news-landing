Chapter 13 - The Dyke Book, Read

There was still the dyke book. Wiebke had not forgotten it — Bo's dying word of the "book in the chest", the transcript in the board archive, which was only an excerpt. The original, the old, leather-bound dyke book that Okke Nissen had kept and Hauke had taken over and Bo had inherited, that had to be still on the farm, in the chest, if no one had thrown it away.

She asked Jan for it. It cost a conversation, for Jan wanted to let the old story rest, but in the end he went up to the attic and brought down the chest, and in the chest, under leases and yellowed letters, it lay: the dyke book, heavy, leather-bound, a hundred years of dyke history. Wiebke opened it, with careful hands, and found the page of 1906, Okke's hand, the entry about the faulty spot, the warning, the dead man. There it was, the original, which Hauke had not torn out, which Bo had not destroyed, which both had left standing, for a hundred years, unread, for the one who would come.

"They didn't destroy it," said Wiebke quietly. "My great-grandfather didn't and my grandfather didn't. They could have torn out the page. No one would have noticed. But they left it standing."

"Why?" asked Jan, who looked over her shoulder.

"I think because they hoped," said Wiebke. "Not aloud, not even admitted to themselves. But they kept the book, passed it on, with the entry in it. As if they had known they were themselves too cowardly to speak it, and hoped that one day someone would come who was not. They didn't erase the truth. They only let it lie, for later. For us."

She handed the dyke book to the county archive, certified, as a historical document, openly accessible. The entry of 1906, unread for a hundred years, was now read, sorted in, part of the documented history of the koog. What Hauke had concealed and Bo had not dared to speak was now named, certified, accessible — no secret any more, only history.

And then Wiebke went one last time out to the new dyke, to the memorial plaque, and looked over the water that now stood over the sunken warft, over the spot where Momme had died, where the guilt had been buried. The water reclaimed it, gently, tide by tide. Out of the secret became again water and watt, tides, a name on a plaque, a page in a book in the archive. Finished. The guilt was not expunged — Hauke was still the one who had saved, Momme still the one who died. But it was named, brought to light, and then given back to the water, not as concealing, but as peace. And that, Wiebke thought, was all a human being could do: not change the past, only stop burying it.
