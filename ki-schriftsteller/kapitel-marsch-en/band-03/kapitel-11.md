Chapter 11 - Reconciliation

The chronicle appeared in winter, a slim volume, published for the depoldering of the Sankt-Annen-Koog, and in it stood, in a chapter on the great storm tide of 1906, the truth that Wiebke and Lena had written down together, soberly, with evidence, without triumph and without whitewash.

It stood in it that the dyke had broken in 1906 at a spot known to be faulty; that the dyke-reeve had been warned and the repair had been left undone out of thrift; that at this spot a man drowned who guarded it, Momme Feddersen; and that the dyke-reeve was called Hauke Carstens. Both names. Momme Feddersen, who was no longer only a victim of misfortune, but a man who died for another's omission. Hauke Carstens, named with what he had done and concealed. The truth, black on white, for anyone who wished to read it.

And there was a memorial plaque. At the new dyke, there where one looked over the depoldered foreland, beneath which the Feddersen warft now lay again under water, one set a plain plaque, and on it stood the name Momme Feddersen, and the year, and a sentence Wiebke and Lena had chosen together: He guarded the dyke that others left to him.

They stood together at the plaque when it was set, Wiebke and Lena, in the cold wind, and looked over the water that now covered the old land.

"I can't forgive you what your family did to mine," said Lena. "That's not for me. Momme would have to forgive, and Momme has been dead a hundred years. And honestly, I don't want to forgive you either, for forgiveness would be too easy."

"I don't want forgiveness," said Wiebke. "I've done nothing I'd need to ask forgiveness for, and for what my great-grandfather did no one can forgive me, because it isn't mine. I only wanted it named. That it stops being hidden. No more."

"That's enough," said Lena. "That's more than I'd ever have expected from a Carstens." And she looked at Wiebke, and in her gaze there was not forgiveness and not enmity, but something new that had never existed between Carstens and Feddersen: respect. "You know what's strange? My brother and your cousin Jan's daughter. They know each other, from school, over in the county town. They're friends, they say. Perhaps more." She smiled, for the first time. "A Feddersen and a Carstens. Imagine."

"Imagine," said Wiebke, and she too smiled, and over the depoldered land, beneath which the old warft and the old guilt now lay honestly under water, the two women shook hands, a Carstens and a Feddersen, and it was no final stroke and no grand reconciliation, but something simpler and more durable: the end of an enmity no one would inherit any more, because the next generation was already overcoming it, without much ado, the way children overcome things that seemed insurmountable to the old.
