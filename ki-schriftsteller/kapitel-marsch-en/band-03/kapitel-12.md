Chapter 12 - The Last Tide at the Old Dyke

The first heavy spring tide after the opening came in February, and it flooded the depoldered foreland full, higher than it had ever stood beneath the old dyke. Wiebke and Jan stood at the new dyke and watched the water rise, over the old land, over the ditches, over the spot where the warft lay, until the whole foreland was a grey, moving surface, sea, where for a hundred years there had been land.

It was a strange hour, one in which something came to an end. The old koog, as three, four generations had known it, had grown smaller; a piece of itself was now sea again. And yet it was no going-under, no breach, no catastrophe like 1906. It was an orderly retreat, a decision, and the new dyke held the water, there where one could and had to hold it.

"The birds are coming," said Jan after a while, almost reluctantly. He had resisted the depoldering to the last, but he was farmer enough to see what was happening: already in the first weeks life had come into the new watt, waders, ducks, and in summer the salt-meadows would bloom, and it would be another fertility than that of the marsh, a wilder, older one. "My grandfather couldn't have borne it," said Jan. "Land one gives back to the water. For him the land was everything."

"Perhaps," said Wiebke. "But perhaps he'd also have understood that the water always wins in the end, and that it's wiser to give it in order than to lose everything uncontrolled. He fought the water all his life. Perhaps he'd have known when a fight is no longer to be won."

They stood in silence, and the water rose, and Wiebke thought of all who had been here: of Hauke, who concealed the dyke and gained; of Momme, who drowned for it; of Bo, who inherited the guilt and repeated it; of Henryk, the foreigner, who died for another; of Thies, who carried the bitterness; of Antje and Grete, the women who knew everything and kept silent. They all had been here, at this dyke, at this water, and they all were gone, and the land they had held and lost and regained now went under water, honestly this time, with their names in the chronicle, on the plaque.

"That's the last tide at the old dyke," said Wiebke. "From now on only the new one counts." And she saw how the water washed over the last remnant of the old dyke, which now lay useless in the watt, a wall that protected nothing any more, a piece of the past the sea reclaimed. The koog, as it had been, ceased to be, and another began, smaller, more honest, with a piece of sea where there had been land, and a truth where there had been silence.
