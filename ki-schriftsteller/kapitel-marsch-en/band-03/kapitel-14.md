Chapter 14 - What the Sea Reclaims

The depoldering at the Sankt-Annen-Koog became a model. Other sections of the threatened coast looked to it, for the problem was everywhere the same: the sea level was rising, the old dykes were not to be held in the long run, and at some point one had to choose where to fight and where to yield. Wiebke was asked, here and there, advised other projects, and she saw that what had happened at the Sankt-Annen-Koog was the beginning of something larger: a rethinking, a different relation to the water, no longer only the struggle for every metre of land, but a giving and taking, a living with the sea instead of only against it.

While at one place an old land was given back to the sea, one built elsewhere new, higher dykes, secured what could be secured. They were two movements at once, the yielding and the strengthening, and both belonged together, both were answers to the same rising water. The coast Wiebke knew was no longer the coast of her forebears; it was in transformation, would transform further, and no one knew exactly how it would look in a hundred years, how much land would remain, how much would become water.

The family, no longer bound to the old soil, scattered further. Jan stayed on the farm, smaller now, without the foreland, but he stayed, for he was a preserver and could not do otherwise. His daughter, though, who was friends with a Feddersen, would perhaps go away, or stay, no one knew. Wiebke herself did not belong here, not any more; she had her life elsewhere, her work along the whole coast, and the Sankt-Annen-Koog was for her now a place of origin, not of home.

It was an ambivalent freedom. For a hundred years the family had clung to this land, to this farm, to this dyke, bound by possession, by duty, by the Spadelandsrecht, by the guilt that lay in the soil. And now the bond was loosed: the foreland was water, the truth was named, the old office of the dyke-reeve no longer existed in that form. The family was free — and freedom was also loss. Whoever is no longer bound to a land has it easier and has less hold. Wiebke felt both, when she thought of the coast that was no longer hers and yet would always remain a part of her.

She stood, on one of her last days at the koog, on the new dyke and looked over the transformed land: the depoldered foreland, now watt and salt-meadow, full of birds; the old dyke, half decayed, washed over by the sea; the warften that still stood, smaller surrounded by water. And she thought that the sea reclaimed what one had taken from it, slowly, patient, over generations, and that it was wiser to understand that than to dyke against it forever. The water rose. The coast remained a task. But it was a more honest task now, one that no longer saw the water only as an enemy, but as what it was: the element out of which this land had come and into which it would return, sooner or later, whether one kept silent or spoke.
