Chapter 5 - Lena Feddersen

Wiebke sought Lena Feddersen a few days after the find, not in the watt, but in the village, where Lena lived in an old house that had once belonged to an aunt. Lena was the last of the Feddersens who had returned to the coast; her parents had gone away, into the west, like so many, and Lena had brought back the biology studies and the love of the watt sea, into a place where her name had a history of which she knew only the outlines.

"I know there's a history between our families," said Wiebke, when they sat in Lena's kitchen, stiff, full of old coldness. "I didn't know it, until I looked into the records. Now I know it. And I think you should know it too."

She laid the transcripts on the table: the protocol of 1906, the entry from the dyke book, the survey files. Lena read. Wiebke watched her read, saw how the other woman's face changed, from coolness to tension to shock. Lena knew the family legend — that her great-grandfather Momme had drowned in the great flood, in the dyke service, and that the family had impoverished afterward while the Carstenses rose, and that there was something, an old grudge, a suspicion no one could prove any more. It had been a tale, a wound without proof. And now the proof lay before her, black on white, brought by a Carstens.

"A faulty spot," said Lena quietly, reading aloud. "The dyke-reeve warned. A dead man at the said spot." She looked up. "My great-grandfather. He guarded the spot your great-grandfather knew would not hold. And he died for it."

"Yes," said Wiebke. "So I read it too."

"Why do you bring me this?" Lena's voice was no longer cool. "You're a Carstens. This incriminates your own family. Why do you dig it up?"

"Because it's true," said Wiebke. "And because in my family there has been silence for a hundred years. My great-grandfather kept silent. My grandfather kept silent, he lay dying and couldn't get it out. As a child I heard him talk of a faulty spot and of Momme, and no one understood it. Now I understand it. And I can't keep silent any more. That would be the fourth. Four generations of silence. At some point someone has to open their mouth."

Lena sat there, the transcript in her hand, and in her face many things struggled: the old grudge, which now had a proof; the shock that the legend was true; and something unexpected, a reluctant respect for the woman opposite her, the Carstens, who had come to bring her own family's guilt to light.

"What do you want to do?" asked Lena.

"I don't know," said Wiebke honestly. "That's why I'm here. It concerns you as much as me. Perhaps more. I didn't want to decide it over your head. It's the history of your great-grandfather. You should have a say in what becomes of it."

And that, Lena thought, that no Carstens had ever done: let a Feddersen have a say. She put the transcript away, slowly, and said: "I have to think about it." And they parted, the two women, without reconciliation, without enmity, with a shared burden that lay between them like the uncovered stones in the watt, and neither of them knew whether quarrel would come of it or peace, but both knew the silence was over.
